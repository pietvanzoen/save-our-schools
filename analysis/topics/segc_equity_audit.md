# SEGC Equity Audit: what it says that matters for rightsizing

**Source:** Green, T. L., & Hanson, H. (2026, June 10). *Equity audit evaluation of Portland Public Schools' (PPS)
Southeast Enrollment and Program Balancing.* 157 pages. PPS commissioned it, and it was presented to the Board at the
Aug 4, 2026 regular meeting [MTG:2026-08-04/758088](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Public/Agenda/915?meeting=758088).
- Main report: [BB:3997427](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915). Page numbers below are PDF pages; they match the printed page numbers.
- Companion report on the 9 case-study schools: *SEGC School by School Analysis* [BB:3997428](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997428?org=915), 130 pages. It is not digested here.

"SEGC" is the Southeast Guiding Coalition. The report uses the name for the whole Phase 2 enrollment and program
balancing effort in Southeast Portland, which was implemented from 2023–24 [BB:3997427 p.3](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=3) [BB:3997427 p.26](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=26).

**Why it matters now:**
- It is PPS's own evaluation of its most recent large reconfiguration.
- PPS central office asked for two of its sections: Section 5, which compares SEGC with research on closures, and
  Section 6, on "well-resourced" schools, QEM and enrollment floors [BB:3997427 p.118](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=118) [BB:3997427 p.133](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=133).
- The report says it wants to inform "future decisions about enrollment balancing, boundary changes, program
  relocation, school consolidation and closures" [BB:3997427 p.3](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=3).

**How to read it.** The lead author asks that it not be used to justify closures: "This report should not be read as a
justification for school closures, consolidation, or any predetermined future rightsizing decision in Portland Public
Schools" [BB:3997427 p.152](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=152). He also says SEGC itself "was not designed or implemented as a school closure initiative"
[BB:3997427 p.106](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=106).

Quotes are verbatim; line breaks in the PDF text are joined. Paragraphs marked "Analysis:" are this archive's
inference, not the report's.

## 1. Bottom line in ten points

1. **The projections were wrong at many schools.** Projections were too high by 20 or more students at 7 of the 20
   SEGC schools and too low by 20 or more at 4 [BB:3997427 p.41](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=41). The report recommends planning with projection
   *ranges* rather than a single number per school [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73).
2. **Most of the "balance" came from schools shrinking.** SEGC "functioned more as a redistribution strategy than as an
   enrollment recovery strategy" [BB:3997427 p.66](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=66).
3. **Test scores and attendance held steady, but that isn't the whole picture.** Outcomes were similar to matched
   peers, with some small positive associations [BB:3997427 p.7](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=7). But families with middle schoolers rated their
   schools much worse, and staff described schools that were "relationally committed but structurally compromised"
   [BB:3997427 p.45](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=45).
4. **Staff were destabilized.** After the implementation year, 32% of SEGC-school staff left their school, compared
   with 19% elsewhere [BB:3997427 p.53](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=53). Staff of color were hit hardest: 39% left, compared with 21% [BB:3997427 p.53](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=53).
5. **Trust is almost gone.** "Only 3% of staff survey respondents mostly or completely trusted the district to handle
   rightsizing fairly, and more than half reported no trust at all" [BB:3997427 p.56](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=56). Among SEGC members, the figure
   is 5% [BB:3997427 p.58](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=58).
6. **The burden fell on poorer schools.** Schools chosen for SEGC averaged 54% free or reduced-price lunch, compared
   with 25% at Southeast schools left out of the process [BB:3997427 p.21](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=21). (The report gives 56% on p.6; see section 10.)
7. **Disruption accumulates.** "districts often evaluate changes one at a time, while schools experience them
   cumulatively" [BB:3997427 p.60](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=60). The report recommends an implementation burden assessment before decisions
   [BB:3997427 p.64](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=64).
8. **Closure savings are usually smaller than expected.** "districts often overestimate the savings produced by school
   closures and underestimate the costs that remain after students leave a building" [BB:3997427 p.147](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=147).
9. **Define "well-resourced" first.** PPS should define it as a concrete, funded staffing model *before* using the
   phrase to justify closures [BB:3997427 p.72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=72). As of early June 2026, PPS had not done so [BB:3997427 p.144](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=144).
10. **Its own size numbers are planning floors, not closure lines.** The report suggests 450 for elementary, 600 for
    middle and about 650 for K–8. It says it "cannot state enough that these numbers should not be used as automatic
    closure thresholds" [BB:3997427 p.145](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=145).

## 2. Enrollment projections and the PSU forecast

- **Size of the misses.** "enrollment projections were overestimated by 20 or more students at seven of 20 SEGC
  schools (35%) and underestimated by at least 20 students at four schools" [BB:3997427 p.41](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=41). The report states the
  counts in other ways too:
  - The executive summary says "SEGC enrollment projections overestimated enrollment at 12 of 20 schools"
    [BB:3997427 p.6](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=6).
  - Footnote 35, using Oct 1 counts, gives 12 schools under and 3 over [BB:3997427 p.41](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=41).
  - The recommendations say "several" schools were more than 50 below and "several" more than 20 above [BB:3997427 p.72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=72).
- **Why the misses matter.** "When enrollment is meaningfully lower or higher than projected, schools may face
  midstream adjustments to staffing, schedules, course offerings, intervention supports, and enrichment
  opportunities" [BB:3997427 p.41](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=41).
- **Student need was underestimated too.** SEGC schools served about 11% more students of color than projected, and
  higher shares of multilingual learners and students in special education [BB:3997427 p.42](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=42). The plan assumed about
  29% low-income; the actual free/reduced lunch rate averaged 56% in 2022–23 [BB:3997427 p.6](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=6).
- **Language programs fell short of their targets.** SEGC dual-language (DLI) enrollment reached 86% of target, about
  280 students below the 1,959 set in 2021 [BB:3997427 p.47](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=47). By language, as a share of target (Table 1.1)
  [BB:3997427 p.48](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=48):

  | Language | Share of target |
  |---|---|
  | Spanish | 104.5% |
  | Japanese | 88.3% |
  | Vietnamese | 70.4% |
  | Mandarin | 68.9% |
  | Russian | 67.3% |

  Middle-school Mandarin was 43% below the plan's forecast of 168 [BB:3997427 p.11](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=11).
- **The recommendation.** "the district should use enrollment projection ranges rather than a single projected number
  for each school. A range-based approach would better reflect uncertainty, avoid creating a false sense of precision,
  and support more responsible planning under changing demographic and fiscal conditions" [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73). Plans
  should show expected, lower-than-expected, higher-than-expected and higher-need scenarios [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73).
- **The PSU forecast.** The report cites the PSU Population Research Center's 2025 forecast [BB:3997427 p.147](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=147). It
  assumes decline "at least until the 2033–34 school year" [BB:3997427 p.144](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=144). It does not test how accurate PSU's
  school-level forecasts are.

Analysis: the 2026 scenario materials still give one projected number per school per year, through 2031-32
[BB:14430171 p.14](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/14430171?org=915#page=14). The Oct 1 Q&A describes "locked" baseline projections as "the foundation of our financial and
operational modeling" [BB:14493920 p.32](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/14493920?org=915#page=32). Neither reflects the audit's recommendation to use ranges. For this
archive's own check of PSU school-level forecast error, see the PSU accuracy work in `analysis/data/` and the
Maplewood page.

## 3. "Well-resourced schools", QEM and size thresholds (Section 6, pp.132–149)

PPS central office asked for this section. The authors say it "should not be read as determining which schools PPS
should close, consolidate, reconfigure, or maintain based on the enrollment numbers and school sizes discussed below"
[BB:3997427 p.133](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=133).

**The report's own definition.** "schools that have the enrollment, staffing, supports, program breadth, class size
conditions, and relational infrastructure necessary to provide students with a complete, stable, and equitable
educational experience" [BB:3997427 p.132](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=132).

**PPS had not defined the term.** "At the time of this writing (early June 2026), PPS has not yet publicly defined the
full staffing support model, program expectations, student support model, or classroom and section structure that will
constitute a well-resourced school in Portland" [BB:3997427 p.144](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=144). Without one, "the phrase “well-resourced school”
risks becoming too vague to guide difficult decisions" [BB:3997427 p.139](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=139).

**Define it before using it.** "PPS should clearly define what it means by a “well-resourced school” before using that
phrase to justify boundary changes, consolidation, closure, or other rightsizing decisions" [BB:3997427 p.72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=72). The
model should be fundable over about five years and should not depend on one-time money [BB:3997427 p.72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=72).

**Recommended order of work** [BB:3997427 pp.139–140](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):
1. Define the staffing model.
2. Derive the number of classrooms or sections needed per grade.
3. Only then set an enrollment range, and test it against funding.

**About QEM's 360-student school.**
- QEM's prototypes are a 360-student elementary school and a 500-student middle school [BB:3997427 p.135](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=135).
- Oregon underfunds its own model. The 2023-25 State School Fund was about $1.5 billion (11.5%) below what QEM called
  for [BB:3997427 p.136](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=136). Fully funding QEM in 2025–27 would take about $2.252 billion more than current service level
  [BB:3997427 p.140](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=140).
- PPS should treat QEM "as a starting floor, not as the final definition of a well-resourced school"
  [BB:3997427 pp.140–141](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).

**Building utilization is not an equity measure.** "utilization rates tell districts something about facilities. They
do not fully tell districts enough about educational equity and adequacy" [BB:3997427 p.135](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=135).

**The report's planning floors** [BB:3997427 pp.144–146](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):
- **Elementary: 450**, meaning QEM's 360 plus about 85 for five years of typical enrollment loss. That is about 75
  students per grade, or three classes of about 25.
- **Middle school: 600.**
- **K–8: about 650.**
- The loss buffer is based on the typical declining PPS school losing about 16 students a year [BB:3997427 p.144](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=144).

These floors are not closure thresholds. "we cannot state enough that these numbers should not be used as automatic
closure thresholds" [BB:3997427 p.145](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=145). Schools below them "may still be necessary, valued, and educationally strong,
but they may require additional district investment, a specialized program model, or an explicit equity rationale"
[BB:3997427 p.146](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=146).

**Research on school size shows trade-offs.** Smaller schools tend to do better on relationships and belonging;
larger schools on breadth of programs. "neither size produces educational quality by itself" [BB:3997427 pp.141–142](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).

Analysis: PPS's scenarios use 360 as the K-5 size threshold, the QEM number the audit calls a floor and not a
definition. PPS has not published the staffing model the audit says must come first.

## 4. Closure research and cost savings (Sections 4–6)

- **Typical savings are small.** About 80% of school spending is salaries and benefits. A rule of thumb from the
  research: "closing 1 of every 15 schools may save about 4% of a district's budget, mostly through labor costs"
  [BB:3997427 p.110](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=110).
- **Savings shrink in practice.** Costs that eat into savings include hiring at receiving schools, transportation,
  renovations and upkeep of empty buildings [BB:3997427 pp.110–111](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915). "If families leave the district because of the
  closure or reconfiguration, the district may also lose enrollment- driven revenue" [BB:3997427 pp.110–111](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- **Estimates tend to be too optimistic.** "districts often overestimate the savings produced by school closures and
  underestimate the costs that remain after students leave a building" [BB:3997427 p.147](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=147), citing Eddins, Pehrson &
  Burgess 2024. The report also cites NEPC 2025 and a Pew study of 12 cities [BB:3997427 p.148](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=148).
- **Empty buildings can take years to sell.** "disposition of school buildings can often take years, and in some cases
  have taken a decade or more" [BB:3997427 p.148](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=148). A disposition plan, holding costs and a timeline should exist
  before closures are final [BB:3997427 p.148](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=148).
- **Publish net savings by category,** and what students gain. PPS should ask “What will be saved, if anything?” but
  also, “What will students receive that they do not currently receive?” [BB:3997427 p.111](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=111).
- **What closures can't fix.** Low birth rates, kindergarten capture, migration, housing costs and school choice:
  "These are not problems a school district can solve through fiscal cuts, consolidations, or closures"
  [BB:3997427 p.147](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=147).
- **Effects on students.**
  - After Chicago's 2013 closures, displaced students' scores fell in the year closures were announced. Students
    already at the receiving schools also dipped in reading. "Reading effects rebounded after one year, but math
    (negative) effects persisted for multiple years" [BB:3997427 p.109](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=109).
  - In Philadelphia, displaced students were absent more often after closures [BB:3997427 p.115](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=115).
- **The standard students should be moved to.** A closure "should be judged by whether students are reassigned to
  schools with demonstrably stronger learning conditions, resources, and the capacity to serve them well"
  [BB:3997427 p.108](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=108).
- **Who is affected.** Majority-Black schools are "far more likely to close than non-majority-Black schools, even
  after accounting for factors commonly used to justify closure" [BB:3997427 p.112](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=112).

## 5. Impacts on students and families

- **Outcome data** [BB:3997427 p.7](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=7) [BB:3997427 p.43](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=43):
  - Test scores, attendance and discipline were similar to matched peers in 2023–24 and 2024–25.
  - There were small positive ELA associations (+0.05 to +0.13 SD) for several groups.
  - Middle schoolers showed no significant differences.
  - These are associations, not proof of cause [BB:3997427 p.34](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=34).
- **Families with middle schoolers rated their schools lower than elementary-only families**
  [BB:3997427 pp.43–45](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):

  | Measure | Elementary-only families | Families with middle schoolers |
  |---|---|---|
  | High-quality learning environment | 61% | 45% |
  | No disrespect or bias | 66% | 48% |
  | Belonging | 78% | 65% |

- **Few families understood the changes.** Only 22% of families said they understood the changes at their school
  [BB:3997427 p.16](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=16).
- **Transportation and access.** Families described long bus rides, unsafe walking routes, and losing after-care and
  sports because of split feeder patterns [BB:3997427 pp.78–79](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915). A parent: "My children get on the bus at 7:06 a.m.…they
  are on the bus for a very, very long time" [BB:3997427 p.78](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=78).
- **Losing community.** "The hardest part for our family was losing our community. My son basically lost all of his
  friendships. We lost our parent group" (a parent) [BB:3997427 p.79](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=79).
- **Displacement inside a program change.** When Lent became a whole-school Spanish DLI school
  [BB:3997427 pp.50–51](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915) [BB:3997427 p.77](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=77):
  - Nearly 60% of students left after 2022–23.
  - "Black students were almost entirely displaced from the school, with the Black student enrollment dropping from
    10% to 2%, while the Latino/a/x share increased from 49% to 69%" [BB:3997427 p.50](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=50).
- **Special education.** Placement depended on whether a classroom had space, and in one case on whether the building
  had a working elevator [BB:3997427 p.83](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=83) [BB:3997427 p.90](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=90). Staff described gaps between need and adult support,
  "especially for students with disabilities, chronic absenteeism, or high behavioral needs" [BB:3997427 p.9](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=9).
- **The downward spiral at small schools.** Lower enrollment leads to staff cuts, which make the school less
  attractive, which loses more students. A Creston parent: "my kid's kindergarten was 15 kids, first grade was like 16
  kids…and then moving to a 35 kid third grade class feels unnecessary" [BB:3997427 p.77](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=77).

## 6. Staff and receiving schools

- **Class sizes.** "only 16% agreed that class sizes are distributed manageably, while 64% said class sizes are too
  large" [BB:3997427 p.46](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=46).
- **Turnover in the implementation year** [BB:3997427 p.53](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=53):
  - 32% of SEGC-school staff left their school, compared with 19% elsewhere. The rates were nearly equal again by
    2024–25 (20% vs 18%).
  - 7 of 13 assistant principals left.
  - Staff of color: 39% left their school, compared with 21% elsewhere, and 18% left the district, compared with 10%
    [BB:3997427 pp.53–54](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- **Principal turnover started earlier.** 55% of SEGC-school principals left in 2021–22, compared with 26% elsewhere
  [BB:3997427 p.52](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=52).
- **Staff were not prepared, then or now.** During implementation, only 6–10% of staff had adequate information,
  support or input. Today, 5% have written guidance for answering families and 4% know whom to contact
  [BB:3997427 p.54](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=54). 25% considered leaving PPS because of SEGC [BB:3997427 pp.55–56](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- **Receiving schools need extra resources.** Participants wanted "schools receiving more students or students with
  greater needs to receive additional resources, not simply be expected to absorb those changes with the same staffing
  and budget" [BB:3997427 p.67](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=67). The research section adds: "receiving schools can experience strain or negative
  spillover effects when they absorb displaced students without sufficient preparation" [BB:3997427 p.114](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=114).
- **Promised support didn't arrive.** Extra staffing promised to affected schools was not delivered: "Our principal had
  to apply to be allowed to request what we were supposedly guaranteed. Year after year she was denied most of it"
  [BB:3997427 p.102](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=102).

Analysis: this archive's class-crowding model (`tools/class_crowding/`,
`analysis/data/class_crowding_model_2026.csv`) estimates the same receiving-school effect for Scenarios A and B.

## 7. Process, engagement and trust

- **The advisory group felt ignored.** "SEGC members largely felt that their input was not meaningfully heard, valued,
  or reflected in final district decisions" [BB:3997427 p.94](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=94). Members said data arrived "20 minutes before the meeting
  that we were supposed to vote on it" [BB:3997427 p.96](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=96).
- **Trust survey of 22 SEGC members** [BB:3997427 p.58](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=58):
  - 23% agreed decision rules were clear.
  - 23% agreed family input was considered.
  - 5% trust the district on rightsizing.
  - The median answer on whether burdens were shared fairly was 1.0, the lowest possible.
- **Feeling heard is not the same as having influence.** "participation alone is not the same as shared power"
  [BB:3997427 p.121](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=121). Engagement "can produce the appearance of inclusion while deepening mistrust" unless the district
  says what is actually open to influence [BB:3997427 p.121](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=121).
- **Wealthier families shouldn't steer the outcome.** "Equity-centered rightsizing cannot be held captive by the
  anticipated departure, resistance, or “exit” of privileged families" [BB:3997427 p.128](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=128).
- **Inconsistent numbers erode trust.** Enrollment and capacity figures shown in public did not match what SEGC had been
  given. "the district must make sure its data is internally aligned before it is shared in public and must correct any
  discrepancies openly and quickly" [BB:3997427 p.100](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=100).

## 8. Cumulative disruption

- **Change piles up.** SEGC landed on top of mergers, program moves, grade changes, construction, the pandemic, strikes,
  budget cuts and leadership turnover: "SEGC was experienced as cumulative and layered rather than as a single
  transition" [BB:3997427 p.23](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=23).
- **The report's framework.** Change Stress = Change Load ÷ Organizational Capacity, on a scale from Stable to Fragile.
  It is meant as a readiness tool, not a school rating [BB:3997427 pp.61–64](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- **Recommendations:**
  - "Future PPS reconfiguration decisions should include an implementation burden assessment before final decisions
    are made" [BB:3997427 p.64](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=64).
  - Conduct a "cumulative equity burden analysis" that identifies which schools have already absorbed boundary
    changes, relocations, reconfigurations, turnover or feeder loss [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73).

## 9. The report's recommendations for any future closure or consolidation

**Before a decision** [BB:3997427 pp.71–73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):
- Be explicit about trade-offs. "A closure may reduce facility costs yet disrupt trusted relationships and student
  belonging" [BB:3997427 p.71](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=71).
- "Create a simple equity impact summary for every school closure or consolidation scenario." It should show students
  affected by race and ethnicity, multilingual status, special education and free/reduced lunch [BB:3997427 p.71](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=71).
- Build scenarios with principals before the public rollout, with guardrails [BB:3997427 pp.71–72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- Define "well-resourced schools" as a funded staffing model [BB:3997427 p.72](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=72).
- Use projection ranges [BB:3997427 pp.72–73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- Conduct a cumulative equity burden analysis [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73).
- "Develop a robust, funded transition plan prior to any Board vote" [BB:3997427 p.73](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=73).

**After a decision:** review staffing and class sizes before school starts, after 30 days, after the first quarter and
at midyear, and add staff when limits are exceeded [BB:3997427 pp.73–74](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).

**Nine essentials from the research** [BB:3997427 pp.113–115](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):
1. Publish criteria before any list of schools.
2. Move students only to clearly stronger schools.
3. Assess and fund receiving schools before implementation.
4. Make multi-year transition plans.
5. Protect staff stability.
6. Treat transportation as an equity issue.
7. Engage early enough to shape the options.
8. Plan for neighborhood impacts and reuse of buildings.
9. Monitor outcomes publicly.

"the most equitable rightsizing process is one that does not begin with a closure list" [BB:3997427 p.115](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=115).

**Questions to answer before any closure** [BB:3997427 pp.148–149](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915):
- Will class sizes rise or fall?
- What specialists will students have access to?
- What will receiving schools need before students arrive?
- What are the transportation burdens?
- How will belonging be protected for racially minoritized students?
- What costs remain for closed buildings?
- How will PPS track whether displaced students end up better off?

**Also:** make future processes districtwide rather than concentrated in some areas [BB:3997427 p.70](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=70), begin by
publicly acknowledging past harms [BB:3997427 p.104](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=104), and promise only supports that are budgeted and publicly tracked
[BB:3997427 p.102](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=102).

Analysis: compared with what PPS has published so far for the 2026 scenarios:
- The equity impact table for students who move is districtwide only [BB:14511742 p.44](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/14511742?org=915#page=44), not school by school.
- No net-savings estimate exists yet; the Board Q&A says savings "can not be determined" [BB:14493920 p.7](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/14493920?org=915#page=7).
- Projections are single numbers, not ranges.
- No staffing model defining "well-resourced" has been published.
- Receiving-school readiness and cumulative-burden analyses have not appeared.
- The superintendent's recommendation and fiscal statement are due Nov 17.

## 10. Methods, scope and caveats

- **Data sources:** all 20 SEGC-affected schools; family survey (n=905); staff survey (n=327); records for 51,983
  students and 4,717 staff; about 50 focus groups and 10 interviews; qualitative case studies at 9 schools
  [BB:3997427 pp.3–5](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915) [BB:3997427 pp.30–33](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
- **Not covered:** students were not interviewed, and central-office staff were not newly interviewed because many
  SEGC leaders had left PPS [BB:3997427 p.3](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=3).
- **Outcome findings are associations, not causes,** because of overlapping events such as COVID, budget cuts and
  strikes [BB:3997427 p.34](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=34).
- **Enrollment counting differs from PPS's.** Students are counted at the school they attended most days, which "is
  not consistent with other reports that rely on the October 1 count" [BB:3997427 p.30](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915#page=30).
- **Internal inconsistencies to watch:**
  - The free/reduced lunch average for SEGC schools is 56% on p.6 and 54% on p.21.
  - Projection-miss counts are stated three ways: pp.6, 41 (including footnote 35) and 72.
  - p.49 says both that families who left relocated DLI programs were "disproportionately English-speaking" and that
    leavers "more often spoke a language other than English at home." p.50 follows the second reading. Don't cite
    either as settled.
- **Authors:** Dr. Terrance L. Green (UT Austin), a researcher on school closures, and Havala Hanson, the quantitative
  lead, based in Portland [BB:3997427 pp.154–157](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/DownloadPDF/3997427?org=915).
