Executive Summary

As part of the required safeguards for transparency and accountability promised to voters in bond language,
the Portland Public School District (PPS) hired Sjoberg Evashenk Consulting (SEC), Inc. in October 2018 to
conduct annual performance audits of the school improvement bonds. For this performance audit cycle, our
audit period covers a two-year period from July 1, 2024, through June 30, 2026.

What We Looked At
Audit objectives focused on:3
   1. Delivery status for the bond program and projects including risks
   2. Contingency practices for the bond program and projects
   3. Implementation status of prior audit recommendations

What We Found
PPS capital delivery teams continue to deliver projects and generally meet obligations to the voters for the
2012, 2017, and 2020 Bonds with extra money made available through bond premiums allowing the completion
of additional projects—more than pledged to the voters. This included six modernized high schools, one middle
school, and one K-8 school in addition to hundreds of planned infrastructure improvements. Infrastructure
projects related to water quality, lead, asbestos, radon, roof, accessibility, seismic retrofit, security, and fire
systems in addition to curriculum and technology upgrades. Also, activity for the 2025 Bond is off to a good
start. Even though it faced challenges over the last thirteen years, PPS has generally delivered within revised
budgets and originally intended timeframes, apart from two important 2020 Bond projects at Jefferson High
School and the Center for Black Student Excellence (CBSE).

While there are always opportunities for improvement that can be made to capital delivery processes and bond
management practices, PPS’ structure and approach employed since 2012 was effective in delivering the bond
program and follow practices and policies that align with industry. Further, PPS made headway implementing
prior annual bond performance audit recommendations with 16 out of 75 total audit recommendations made
over the past six years remaining in progress. Eight of the 16 in-progress recommendations were only first
reported in the annual bond performance audit finalized in June 2025.

Despite this progress, this audit revealed fiscal controls that need to be strengthened as we found unallowable
bond expenditures that did not align with authoritative accounting treatment, payroll charges lacking supporting
documentation, potential missed savings opportunities, contract overpayments that did not comply with contract
terms, and additional fiscal impacts from practices as shown in Exhibit 1. After auditors brought these issues
to management’s attention, PPS began implementing corrective actions, controls, and processes.

Further, as the audit began, our risk assessment revealed higher risks, repeated claims, and significant press
attention placed on management’s reorganization of its bond program and related contracts. These risks and
reports related to outsourcing, staff terminations, external collaborations, competitive solicitations, and contract
awards. Given the concerns raised by internal PPS staff, external stakeholders, and the press, our audit
standards require us to examine these matters.4

This audit revealed that PPS leadership took several actions that increased risks to the bond program without
requisite controls or activities to mitigate those risks. For instance, PPS leadership made significant shifts in its
bond delivery approach and terminated the employment of almost half of the internal positions that made up
its capital delivery program, impacting institutional knowledge and expertise gained over the prior thirteen years
of the bond and replacing the work performed by those positions with contracted services. PPS experienced
significant leadership turnover in the bond program, including the terminated bond delivery staff whose
responsibilities will now be largely performed by an external consultant hired to manage the bond program and
provide construction support services. While management and the Board have the discretion to outsource

First, PPS lacked a clear and cohesive business case for the shift. There was no evidence provided that
outsourcing the bond program was more cost-effective than keeping the activities in-house to support the need
for change or that the change in approach would be more effective and significantly alter the positive results of

managing contacts, indicated that a different and more targeted management approach was appropriate.

Second, the decision to outsource was made without sufficient and reliable information available to the Board
and the public. Management asserted that there were concerns with the bond program and Board leadership
stated the bond program was a failure; yet over the past six years our audits have demonstrated that the bond
program has delivered projects as approved by the voters. There was a lack of substantive and accurate
information provided to the Board upon which to make decisions regarding the external consultant contract with
both Board and PPS leadership making assertions about bond program performance, project status, and
condition of bond program that contradicted previous independent audit results. Management conveyed a
sense of urgency regarding the need for the Board to quickly approve contracts for interim bond leadership and
program management. This urgency was predicated on assertions that the capital delivery team had failed to
deliver on the bond program—which was not accurate. According to PPS, the urgency was also driven by a
lack of leadership within OSM with two significant resignations and PPS had significant concerns about the
ability to execute three modernization projects concurrently based on its reliance on OSM leadership’s
assertions.

Nonetheless, this sense of urgency led the Board to act quickly, without full due diligence to make an informed
decision, such as a clear business case for the reorganization and decision to outsource, the cost-benefit to
PPS, and the methods management would employ to ensure bond performance under the outsourced
approach would exceed the performance of in-house staff. Some Board members asked for additional
information and extra time to review this information, but the Board voted on the contracts before such
information was made available. This, in turn, revealed disconnects between the Board’s view of its bond
oversight and fiduciary responsibilities, including internal controls for the bond program, and best practices for
effective governance.

Third, PPS terminated a significant portion of its bond program personnel, resulting in a substantial loss of
institutional knowledge, and did not incorporate personnel with capital expertise to oversee and monitor the
performance and compliance of its consultant in the newly outsourced model.

Introduction and Background

As the largest K-12 public school district in Oregon with slightly more than 40,000 students and 81 schools,
PPS has been working on modernizing its aging school facilities and upgrading student learning environments
through funding provided by general obligations bonds.6 To date, PPS voters in Multnomah and Washington
County have passed four bond measures for school improvements in 2012, 2017, 2020, and 2025. 7 Combined,
these bonds authorized $4.3 billion in funding through a property tax levy for modernizing school facilities. PPS
received additional revenues through bond premiums, interest earnings, and grants increased the funding
available across the bonds to nearly $4.7 billion, as shown in Exhibit 2.

School Capital Improvement Bond Program
In 2012, the bond centered on school improvement efforts at Grant, Franklin, and Roosevelt High Schools in
addition to Faubion PK-8 School; while the 2017 bond largely focused on Lincoln, McDaniel, and Benson
Polytechnic High Schools in addition to Kellogg Middle School as shown in Exhibit 2. Both bonds also set aside
funds for a series of accessibility, seismic, and health and safety improvements at multiple schools within PPS.

With the passage of a third bond in 2020, PPS expanded planned areas to include not only traditional capital
improvements at physical school buildings such as the modernization of Jefferson High School but also allowed
funding for educational curriculum and information technology infrastructure improvements as well as the CBSE
also known as the Grice-Adair Center for Student Excellence.

Additionally, the 2025 Bond included implementing the design and construction for the modernizations of
Cleveland and Ida B. Wells High Schools, added funding to complete Jefferson High School, and authorized
PPS to utilize cost savings to perform updates or repairs to elementary and middle schools. The bond also
included funding for additional curriculum, technology, and infrastructure improvements to seismic, safety,
athletic fields, and performing arts.

Bond Capital Projects Budget versus General Fund Budget
Bond capital programs are complex, challenging, and long-term involving changing conditions, heavy reliance
on supply markets, exposure to inflationary pressures, and higher levels of public scrutiny and accountability,
requiring the need for full transparency and clear, accurate financial communications.

Although the spending of bond money spans several years, PPS must budget annually for expected
expenditure and debt service repayment against estimated bond proceeds.8 This activity is included in PPS’
capital projects fund (that also includes smaller non-bond capital projects) and PPS’ debt service fund. Over
the last six years, the capital projects fund budget averaged about half of PPS’ entire budget, as shown in
Exhibit 3. Conversely, the general fund budget averaged less than 38 percent over the same period. Bond
funds must only be spent on capital improvements identified in the bond measures and cannot be used for
general operations.

Bond Program Management and Individual Project Management
Capital improvements involve a multitude of tasks, stakeholders, and variables that challenge a project or
program’s ability to stay on schedule, within budget, and aligned with planned scope. To ensure bond projects
are managed and implemented as pledged to voters, PPS employs both program management activities and
project management activities. While bond program management—focuses on a macro, strategic level across
a portfolio of several projects, project management and delivery focuses on the micro, day-to-day
implementation of an individual project. This is illustrated in Exhibit 4.

These project delivery teams also involve construction managers or external construction management firms
to perform site inspections, process submittals and requests for changes to the contract, review contractor
payment applications, and monitor general contractor compliance with contract responsibilities. Together, this
delivery team is critical for providing close oversight over a contractor’s performance and adherence to the
contract.

Modernizing aging school facilities involves a variety of PPS executives and staff, private sector consultants
and contractors, external stakeholders, a community-led accountability committee, and an elected oversight
board that work together on ensuring bond programs are delivered as planned as shown in Exhibit 5.

Since the first bond was approved in 2012, PPS assigned to the Office of School Modernization (OSM) the
responsibility for administering, managing, and implementing capital improvement projects. This remained
OSM’s responsibility until December 2025. OSM executed the design and construction of the school

meetings of the Board’s FIO committee and BAC, and status reporting to the Board. In early 2026, PPS
reorganized the bond delivery structure into the new Real Estate, Infrastructure, and School Enhancement
(RISE) department. Overseen by the PPS’ Senior Chief of Operations, the Executive Director position for the
RISE department was outsourced to an external firm that has assumed responsibility for bond program
management and implementing the high school bond modernization projects and capital improvements related
to CBSE. While RISE primarily focuses on the bond program, it is also responsible for real estate, space
planning, and non-bond funded smaller capital projects. These other functions are managed by a PPS Senior
Director. According to PPS, once a newly advertised position for Executive Director of Capital and Planning is
filled, the RISE structure will look different.

Additional PPS departments became involved with the management and implementation of bond areas outside
of typical capital construction projects. This includes the Office of Technology & Information Services (OTIS)
for technology projects and the Office of Teaching & Learning (OTL) for curriculum projects. The bond program

procuring external firms to assist with bond-funded capital projects, Finance helping with bond financing and
interpreting accounting principles for bond compensability, and Career and Technical Education (CTE) working

Since the 2012 Bond passed, OSM has delivered nearly $2.2 billion in capital projects to modernize six full high
schools, one middle school, and one PK-8 modernization. Also, OSM has delivered hundreds of improvements
related to accessibility, lead paint, water, asbestos, seismic, roof, security, mechanical, and technology
infrastructure, as well as curriculum programs, as pledged to voters. Another $643 million remains available
from bond premiums and interest earned which will allow additional projects to be delivered for the 2012, 2017,
and 2020 Bond projects. With the passage of the 2025 Bond, an additional $1.8 billion in funding is available
for projects that are starting up and off to a good start.10 RISE continues to make progress and meet bond
obligations to voters, as did its predecessor, OSM.

Prior annual bond performance audits issued between 2014 and 2025 reported that OSM employed delivery
protocols over the bond program that aligned with best practices and industry standards—although auditors
recommended improvements as is common in audits of large-scale capital programs.11 OSM implemented
many of these prior audit recommendations; in fact, of the 75 recommendations auditors made since 2019,
only 16 audit recommendations remain to be implemented.12

The previous OSM Program Management Plan contained detailed instructions on how to establish contingency
when a new project was set up. Specifically, budgets must include 15 percent for contingency on renovation
projects and 10 percent for new construction—thresholds that align with industry. The formal guidance also
required staff to move any remaining project funds upon close out to bond program contingency, which holds
unallocated bond funds until assigned to specific projects. Since contingency amounts can be substantial on
large capital projects (e.g. high school modernizations), having a formal plan to guide decisions on contingency
reallocations once a project is complete is important to ensure available funds are used on other bond-eligible
improvements.

Like OSM’s previous guidance, RISE also has formal plans that stipulate any unused funds (after final project
costs are identified) should go back to program contingency. For example, it establishes a contingency of 5
percent for construction projects greater than $200 million and 10 percent for construction projects less than
$200 million. The plan also states that funds will be allocated to complete authorized bond program projects
first, and only later fund other improvements from priority lists to meet subsequently identified needs.
Additionally, RISE plans specify “strategies” surrounding contingency thresholds as embedded in its policy
which defined standardized contingency budgets for various bond projects.

Yet, neither the new framework to manage the bond program nor the contingency strategies specify processes
or controls for how RISE will reallocate and prioritize the unused program contingency for new project
improvements in alignment with bond provisions. For instance, as highlighted in the prior annual bond
performance audit issued in June 2025, the Lincoln High School modernization project completed as part of
the 2017 Bond had nearly $17 million in unused budget. OSM intended to move those “savings” to program
contingency for reallocation and use on other 2017 Bond projects. To date, those funds remain in the project’s
contingency budget with no action to move the amount to the program contingency budget. There are also no
documented decisions on how projects or improvements will be prioritized or which improvements should be
included.

With a total balance of over $25 million in 2017 bond contingency, RISE might be able to complete more
projects if there were a methodical process in place. Without formal protocols or quantifiable prioritization
criteria to aid project managers and RISE leadership when making end-of-project contingency reallocation
decisions, millions of available bond contingency funds may not be utilized in the most effective manner to
complete project improvements.

Stable, long-tenured executive leadership that supports and oversees bond activities along with project delivery
staff provides the best chance for positive outcomes. While turnover can negatively impact an agency’s ability
to fulfill long-term bond programs and commitments made to voters, it cannot be completely avoided.50

Prior annual bond performance audits found significant turnover in executive and upper-level management.
Between 2017 and 2018, the Chief Operating Officer position and other chief-level positions in finance and
technology (which oversee bond projects) turned over multiple times. When past OSM and executive level

remaining staff to keep momentum toward completing bond projects as planned while staff recruitment and
replacement efforts were underway. Although recurring turnover, and the resulting loss of institutional
knowledge or shifts in priorities, can be challenging, OSM’s staffing approach, methods, and controls remained
consistent. This helped to ensure bond projects were delivered as pledged to the voters.

Finding 4: Certain Activities and Assertions Made Before and During

Due to the concerns raised during the course of our annual bond performance audit and as part of our
assessment of risk, we reviewed the bond procurement of the outsourced service provider and bond program
management. We found that PPS did not employ sufficient controls to ensure the solicitation and award process
was not compromised.

PPS’ outsourced bond delivery approach centered around two contracts with an external vendor, Procedeo.
One contract’s scope was for recommendations to accelerate the Jefferson High School project, suggestions
for a staffing structure, and provision of interim leadership for OSM. The other contract was for bond program
management services including project management, construction management, and adminstrative oversight
of OSM functions. During fall 2025, when PPS presented contracts to the Board for approval, PPS leadership
made statements surrounding the need and urgency of board decisions to approve the bond contracts that
were inaccurate and urged the Board to rush approval of the contracts limiting the timeline available for Board
due diligence. Discussions were supported by inaccurate messaging regarding the consultant’s unique
expertise, safeguards provided in contract language, and the bond program’s past performance and status.
These assertions contradicted prior audit results, and information presented in Finding 1 of this report. Relying
on inaccurate information upon which to make decisions increases risk that those decisions might not be in the
best interest of taxpayers. According to PPS, their assertions were based on management concerns about the
future execution of the bond program and its assessment of concerns raised by OSM.

Prior to contract solicitation, review, award, and board approval, PPS communicated about its intent and plans
with the consultant that was ultimately awarded the contracts. Communication included planning discussions
related to proposed bond management services, potential reorganization of the bond delivery structure, and
how the consultant could help PPS—which could have influenced PPS, giving the consultant an unfair
advantage during the solicitation, evaluation, and award process. Although PPS asserted that it talked with
other consultants on similar topics, PPS did not provide documentary evidence to support this assertion.
Management’s actions work against and diminish the effectiveness of internal controls and safeguards that
should protect the integrity of the procurement process from improper influence.

Additionally, PPS’ scoring of written proposals for the bond management services competitive solicitation did
not capture the fact that one consultant did not fully address the “firm experience” evaluation criteria. When
combined with discrepancies in PPS’ approval of the consultant’s invoice post-award as discussed later in
Finding 5 of this report, these actions and events increase risk that the process was compromised and could
have been influenced by favor or bias. These actions conflict with the purpose of Board Policy 5.10.066-P to
perform district business without the perception of undue influence and its code of ethics to avoid the

On behalf of the public, the Board has full authority over the district and total accountability for the district
including the bond program. 131 Although the Board relies on the Superintendent to implement policies and day-
to-day operations, the Board is still responsible for how the district works, its internal control framework, and
for overseeing whether PPS is meeting its goals and obligations to the public through an effective control
structure. The prior annual bond performance audit found PPS employed a typical oversight framework with
the Board holding work sessions that discussed modernization projects and bond planning as well as engaged
in discussions surrounding other bond topics related to construction, costs, schedule, contracting, equity, and
more. Past audit recommendations were made to improve governance, and board members have actively led
changes to committee charters and policies strengthening oversight to address the audit recommendations.

However, that framework broke down during fall 2025 when we found instances where the Board did not fully
exercise its bond oversight and fiscal responsibility over the bond program management services contract
impacting accountability to the public it serves. Specifically, the Board did not allow its members to obtain critical
information requested as part of due diligence related to the bond program management contract.132
Additionally, the Board’s view of its bond fiduciary responsibilities, oversight, and governance related to its role,
the role of management, and the role of internal audit do not align with best practices.

Given that the bond capital projects comprise an average 50 percent of the district budget over the past six
fiscal years, it is critical that the Board strengthens fiscal oversight over spending taxpayer bond funding on
significant contracts, PPS’ capital budget, and the bond program overall. During the first half of 2026, the Board
has been actively working on governance through a series of externally led retreats and self-evaluations as
part of its current strategic planning efforts. Additionally, the Board has changed its practices related to member
inquiries, improving its process related to exercising due diligence.

Appendix A: Scope and Methodology

School improvement bonds passed by voters require annual audits of bond projects and expenditures. To fulfill
that requirement, PPS engaged Sjoberg Evashenk Consulting, Inc. in October 2018 to conduct annual
performance audits of the school improvement bonds. For the current performance audit cycle, our audit period
covers a two-year period from July 1, 2024, through June 30, 2026.

Our objectives were as follows:
    1. Bond Budget, Schedule, and Scope Status
       Identify the delivery status of the Bond projects in terms of cost, schedule, and high-level scope against
       bond pledges, in addition to assessing risks and challenges with the bond program and projects.
    2. Bond Program Contingency Management and Usage
       Assess whether PPS has a clear, documented process in place to manage, control, and use
       contingency for the bond program.
    3. Implementation Status of Prior Audit Recommendations
       Determine whether PPS has sufficiently addressed prior audit recommendations related to bond
       activities and implemented satisfactory corrective action through verification of actions taken.

To identify and assess risk as well as inform audit procedures, we conducted multiple interviews with more
than 50 individuals meeting on multiple occasions as follows:
    •   Oversight: PPS Board members and BAC members
    •   Executive Leadership: Senior Chief of Operations, Chief Financial Officer, Chief of Staff, and
        Contracted Senior Legal Advisor
    •   Office of Internal Performance Audit: Senior Internal Performance Auditor
    •   Former OSM: Senior Director, Directors of Construction, Senior Project Managers, Manager of
        Financial Services, Project Managers, and contracted Construction Managers
    •   Former Real Estate & Construction: Senior Director and Director Project Management & Construction
    •   RISE: Executive Director, Senior Director, Deputy Director, Senior Construction Manager, Senior
        Design Manager
    •   OTIS: Chief Technology Officer, Senior Director of Technology Operations, and Communications and
        Change Manager
    •   OTL: Senior Director of Instructional Excellence & Improvement and Manager of Instructional
        Resources Adoption
    •   CSE: Senior Director of Family & Community Engagement
    •   Other management and staff including the Chief of Integrated Operations, Director of Purchasing and
        Contracting, Senior Contracts Analyst, Senior Accountant Bond Accountant in Finance, Budget
        Director in Finance, and Senior Director of Human Resources Operations.
    •   External contractors involved in 2017 Bond and 2020 Bond projects and other external bond
        stakeholders.

We completed work to assess internal controls significant to our audit, including controls over project delivery,
program management, staffing, expenditures, payroll, contracting and procurement, data reporting, and
oversight and governance. We reported our conclusions throughout the report in Findings 1 through 6.

To assess the overall bond status, progress, and performance of the 2012 Bond, 2017 Bond, 2020 Bond, and
2025 Bond projects in terms of cost, schedule, and high-level scope for adherence with the bond pledges and
planning documents, we:
    •   Analyzed the schedule delivery status and budget status by reviewing underlying cost and schedule
        estimate at completion from the e-Builder system project files in addition to materials and reports
        presented to oversight groups.
    •   Compared the type and quantity of projects OSM planned as described in bond documents or internal
        facility plans against those projects OSM completed for each “category type” of improvement for each
        bond cycle and assessed high-level delivery progress.
    •   Reviewed baseline schedules and budgets to compare delivery status and costs to initial plans.
    •   Compared actual costs and schedules against planned budgets and schedules from internal OSM
        planning documents at the time the bond ballot measure was put before the voters.
    •   Conducted high-level review of significant variances in budget and schedule using project file data
        such as Owner-Architect-Contractor meeting minutes, project status reports, schedule reports,
        requests for information, change orders, and other project files. Investigated significant variances in
        cost or schedule to determine PPS steps to minimize risk.

To evaluate fiscal controls over bond expenditures and contingency practices, we:
    •   Assessed fiscal controls in place to ensure project eligibility, allowability of costs, expenditure reviews
        and approvals, and audits of expenditures aligned with the bond measure and best practice.
    •   Evaluated contingency policies and practices for managing, controlling, and using contingency for the
        bond program, including identifying and confirming the amount of funds in bond contingency.
    •   Identified project prioritization and selection protocols to determine if OSM had selected and executed
        projects according to criteria outlined by PPS and bond language.
    •   Reviewed processes at a high-level for tracking expenditures and the timing of bond spending and
        cash flow for impact on contingency usage.
    •   Tested project, payroll, and contract expenditures for allowability and approval, against underlying
        supporting invoices, bond language, and legal and accounting guidance.
    •   Considered authoritative legal and accounting principles including the Oregon constitution, IRS
        regulations, general obligation bond language, and industry guidance to define capital assets and test
        bond compensability.
    •   Reviewed and interpreted relevant accounting principles and statements including Generally Accepted

To identify the status of prior audit recommendations and evaluate PPS’ progress toward implementing
corrective action, we:
    •   Verified auditee implemented actions for recommendations noted as closed during our audit period by
        confirming against relevant documents and fieldwork analyses.
    •   For recommendations noted as still open and not yet addressed during our audit period, we assessed
        status and progress toward implementation. Where applicable, we considered the rationale and
        challenges faced with not implementing recommendations.

In performing this audit, we relied on electronic data files obtained from the district for testing program status
(e-Builder system), contracts (Contract Management System, or CMS), and expenditures (PeopleSoft financial
system). Prior audits validated information from the e-Builder system against source documents, and external
auditors test the reliability of financial data from the PeopleSoft system as part of annual Single Audits. The
CMS system was used to gather statistics and is not considered significant to our audit objectives. Thus, there
is sufficient evidence in total to support our audit findings, conclusions, and recommendations.

We conducted this performance audit in accordance with generally accepted government auditing standards.
Those standards require that we plan and perform the audit to obtain sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide
a reasonable basis for our findings and conclusions based on our audit objectives. We believe that the evidence
obtained provides a reasonable basis for our findings and conclusions based on our audit objectives.

 We Disagreed with PPS’ Assessment in a Few Instances
 For the most part when validating efforts to address prior audit recommendations, we agreed with and verified
 PPS’ assessment of actions taken to implement and close an audit recommendation. However, there were
 several instances where we did not agree that PPS-implemented activities sufficiently addressed a
 recommendation as follows.


 SJOBERGEVASHENK                                                                                                                          P a g e | 121
    ×   Year 4, Rec #2: Develop Protocols for Reviewing and Assessing Workforce Equity Strategies
        PPS stated it had integrated an annual review of workforce equity strategies into its broader annual
        report developed. Although the annual equity report reported outcomes, it did not discuss protocols for
        reviewing the strategies on a designated timeline as recommended.
    ×   Year 4, Rec #5: Revisit and Report on Key Performance Indicators
        PPS closed the recommendation stating it had been "overcome by events” as “PPS outsourced bond
        program management to Procedeo.” Yet, the recommendation focused on project specific indicators
        such as days between planned substantial completion to actual (for schedule), number and amount of
        change order compared to contract value, and percent of projects on budget at completion (for budget).
        While the Procedeo PMP states it will track schedule variance, budget variance, contingency burn rate,
        and stakeholder satisfaction among others, those metrics are not yet in place or reported.
    ×   Year 6, Rec # 4: Establish and Complete Contactor Evaluations
        Although PPS has partially addressed this recommendation and established a contractor evaluation
        template with questions to consider, it still needs to establish the surrounding procedure for who is
        responsible for completing the evaluation, when it is given, and how it will be used in future
        procurements—in addition to completing the evaluations for the most recent completed contracts.
    ×   Year 6, Recs #12 and 13: Staff Time Tracking, and Workload and Staff Capacity Analysis
        Former OSM staff agreed that regular time studies for staff spending less than 100 percent of time on
        the bond was reasonable, but RISE staff indicated “all bond funded employees spend 100 percent of
        the time on bond work” so PPS closed the recommendation. Yet, as shown in Finding 6 of this report,
        we found several staff partially charged to the bond. Likewise, PPS indicated a different
        recommendation to conduct a staffing analysis had been "overcome by events” as “PPS outsourced
        bond program management to Procedeo." Yet, there was no staff analysis done; thus, the item remains
        open along with new related recommendations as discussed in Finding 4 of this report.

We Closed Out One Recommendation Because Prior Documents are Unlikely to be Found
For a final recommendation related to post-project analysis, we considered it complete because documents
are lost due to staff turnover and it does not seem practical to conduct a project analysis when some staff and
leadership on the past projects are no longer working at PPS—and a similar recommendation (Year 6, Rec #1)
remains open.
    ✓ Year 2, Rec #7: Post Project Analysis on Madison and Lincoln High Schools
      PPS reported it made progress on this recommendation by developing evaluation templates,
      conducting analysis, and preparing a report for Lincoln High School. However, since PPS terminated
      staff involved with the school modernizations in January 2026, the new RISE team cannot locate the
      documentation. Conducting post-mortems on the large-scale modernizations is critical for lessons
      learned to be incorporated on subsequent projects. Thus, we will continue to track a similar outstanding
      recommendation (Year 6, Rec #1) calling for a post-mortem on the Benson High School project—
      although PPS should have completed that effort before starting the Cleveland, Ida B. Wells, and
      Jefferson High School modernizations to maximize value. Nonetheless, we considered this
      recommendation completed.

    7
          PPS’ narrative response to Recommendation 17 focuses on actual and potential conflict of
 Page 133 interest definitions as established in Oregon ethics law and its own Public Contracting Rules but
          does not address perceived conflicts of interest. For this performance audit, auditors assessed
          risks related to procurement and contracting, including perceived conflicts of interest, in addition
          to relevant internal controls to mitigate those risks.
             Perceived conflicts of interest differ from actual or potential conflicts and happen when a
             reasonable person may view a particular set of circumstances or events and question whether
             bias influenced an outcome—regardless of whether there was actual bias or conflict. They can
             undermine trust and public confidence—often just as much as if a real conflict existed. As
             described in Finding 4 on page 69, government entities need to take precautions to avoid
             situations where the public could view a public official’s actions as giving an unfair advantage to
             one proposer over another, even if individuals act fairly and unbiased in their decision-making
             and an entity followed laws and its internal policies.
             PPS’ narrative response to Recommendation 17 states that prior discussions or prolonged
             previous dealings is not a basis for dismissal from an evaluation committee and does not require
             disclosure. However, given the sensitivity surrounding bond funds and public scrutiny of district
             bond actions, PPS did not employ sufficient controls to protect the integrity of and mitigate risks
             related to the bond program management consultant selection process as described in Finding
             4 on pages 63 through 69.
             While PPS may disagree with our findings and conclusions, Recommendation 17 relates to
             strengthening processes and tightening accountability to reduce risks of perceived conflicts in
             future competitive solicitations, ensuring evaluation instructions are clear, and clarifying
             instructions surrounding actual, potential, or perceived conflicts. All elements of strong
             procurement control and best practices, especially given the audit found (1) unclear evaluation
             instructions for how evaluators assign weight when one RFP category has multiple components
             to be evaluated but only one score and (2) circumstances that increase risk of the perception of
             conflicts of interest.
