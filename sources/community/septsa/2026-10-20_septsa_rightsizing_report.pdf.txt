This report was prepared by the PPS Special Education Parent Teacher Student Association
(SEPTSA), with input from families, for the PPS Rightsizing Committee, Superintendent, and
PPS Board of Education. It is being delivered on Tuesday, October 20, 2026.


Executive Summary
PPS's proposed Rightsizing scenarios raise significant concerns about disproportionate
impacts on students with disabilities, particularly those with the highest support needs
or who are multiply-marginalized. These concerns extend beyond school closures and focus
classroom relocations – they include disabled students receiving services in general education,
Learning Centers, Inclusive Schools, and other settings. SEPTSA is requesting the data,
analysis, and explanations needed to understand these impacts, assess whether students'
needs would be met under either scenario, and evaluate alternatives before decisions are
made.

Introduction
Students with disabilities, especially those who are Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual
learners, or experiencing poverty, prove their resilience every day. They are more likely to be
bullied by peers. They are more likely to be shuffled between placements and buildings, or to be
barred from attending school with their siblings and neighbors. And they are more likely to
experience exclusionary discipline, from losing recess to being suspended. Still, they (and their
families) keep showing up, insisting that their gifts and humanity be seen, and building
belonging and community in spite of it all.

This report outlines SEPTSA’s collective concerns and explains why the information PPS has
provided is insufficient to determine whether any proposed Rightsizing scenario would preserve
equitable access to appropriate educational settings, services, and supports for students with
disabilities.


Priority 1: Districtwide Service Inventory and
Disability Impact
 The district says “services follow students,” so families and the Board need to see the
 current landscape of special education services and students. The share of students
 affected by closures doesn’t show whether the plan is equitable for the full range of
 students with disabilities. That takes a disability-specific analysis of all scenarios, with
 particular attention to students at the intersections of disability, poverty, race, or
 multilingual learner status.

 ⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that the proposal would further concentrate more
 students with IEPs at higher-poverty receiving schools (see Appendix A: Disability & Poverty).

Students with disabilities are already moved between buildings and placements more than other
students, before any rightsizing change. Focus classrooms exist at only 34 of PPS’s 73
elementary, K-8, and middle schools, so most students placed in them attend school outside
their neighborhood, apart from siblings and peers. Programs don’t always continue across
grade bands at the same site, and students may move between placements. Rightsizing would
add new disruptions on top of these existing ones, affecting students in focus classrooms as
well as those receiving services through Learning Centers, Inclusive Schools, and general
education.

This is especially true for disabled students who are also Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual
learners, or experiencing poverty. Ableism does not act alone; it compounds existing inequities.
SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that Scenario A would concentrate more students with
IEPs at higher-poverty receiving schools, including Rosa Parks, Whitman, and Sitton (see
Appendix A: Disability & Poverty). PPS must verify these projections and examine how the

Priority 2: Pathways, Program Continuity, and
Transitions
 The current proposed scenarios do not attend to the diverse needs of disabled
 students, and in particular the needs that IEP minutes can’t capture. For some
 students with disabilities, particularly those with sensory needs, anxiety, or trauma
 histories, a smaller setting or reduced transitions is what makes their IEP possible to
 implement.

 ⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that the thematic focus options losing grades or
 their program serve the highest shares of students with IEPs among PPS focus options, from
 22% to 31%, compared with a middle school average of 16% (see Appendix B: Secondary
 School Options for Disabled Students).

Both scenarios propose to eliminate most of PPS’s K-8 middle grades, the middle grades at two
thematic focus options, and MLC’s alternative high school, consolidating students into larger
comprehensive schools. These are among the settings that serve the highest shares of students
with disabilities (see Appendix B: Secondary School Options for Disabled Students). For many
of those students, they are where public education has finally worked. The proposal treats a
smaller, continuous environment as an inefficiency. For some students with disabilities, it is how
they access a free appropriate public education. Disabled and neurodivergent students also
face far higher risks of bullying and suicide. For them, these settings are not a luxury. They are
protection.

their neighborhood school. Some focus classrooms don’t offer K-5 continuity, so students

their school communities and peers.

Priority 3: Staffing, Caseloads, and Service Capacity
 The District presents rightsizing as a path to more stable staffing. Families, staff, and
 the Board need to know what that means for special education specifically: who will
 serve students and at what caseloads.

We understand the benefits of a well-resourced school, and we value the proposal's goal:
consistent, stable staffing dedicated within each building; balanced class sizes across schools;
and access to support staff like counselors and social workers. Consolidating schools could
improve access to special education staff and specialists, but PPS needs to show how that
would work in practice. Staffing numbers alone don't tell us whether students will receive the
services they need. The district must account for vacancies, caseloads, service schedules,
general education class sizes, and the support students need throughout the school day.

Priority 4: Building Capacity, Space, and Accessibility
 The District often says special education is “a service, not a place.” But services are
 delivered somewhere. Families and staff need assurance that receiving schools will
 have the classrooms, regulation spaces, and accessible facilities students need, and
 that services won’t end up in hallways, storage rooms or shared offices.

 ⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis suggests that many receiving elementary schools may not
 have enough rooms to meet projected needs (see Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility
 Utilization).

PPS cannot determine whether a school has room for additional students based on building
capacity alone. The district must identify the classrooms, therapy and related-service spaces,
accessible restrooms, and sensory regulation areas students need. PPS must also explain how
it will preserve these spaces as enrollment increases, rather than converting them into
additional classrooms at the expense of students with disabilities.

PPS's Long Range Facility Plan (Portland Public Schools, 2021) identified inadequate special
education spaces and established space standards. PPS must explain which standards still
apply and how it used them when developing the Rightsizing scenarios. SEPTSA's preliminary
analysis suggests that seven of the eleven receiving elementary schools under Scenario A may
not have enough rooms to meet projected needs. PPS must verify these findings using current




 SEPTSA | PPS Rightsizing                 October 2026                              Page 9 of 36
room inventories, existing room uses, projected enrollment, and applicable facility standards
(see Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility Utilization).

Priority 5: Sensory Impact & Meaningful Inclusion
 For many students with disabilities, the sensory environment is part of how their
 disability is experienced. Larger schools mean more noise, crowding, and transitions.
 Families and the Board need to know whether and how the district assessed this in
 selecting closing and receiving schools.

 ⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis suggests that proposed enrollment increases could place
 disproportionate pressure on Inclusive School Communities (see Appendix D: Inclusive
 School Communities).

Larger schools may offer more resources, but increased noise, crowding, and daily transitions
can create barriers for students with disabilities. PPS must evaluate how each scenario would
affect students' ability to learn, regulate, build relationships, and participate in school activities.
That means examining the staffing, routines, instructional approaches, and inclusion supports
that currently work for students, along with what receiving schools could provide. Smaller
schools and focus-option programs are not automatically better settings, but PPS must account
for what makes them effective for the students they serve.

SEPTSA's preliminary analysis of Scenario A projects that enrollment at four Inclusive Schools
receiving communities would average 34% above a 360-student benchmark, compared with
20% above that benchmark at other receiving K-5 schools (see Appendix D: Inclusive School
Communities). PPS must verify these calculations and explain how it uses the 360-student
benchmark in Rightsizing decisions. The district must also assess how larger enrollments could
affect students' sensory environments, access to inclusion supports, and availability of services.
Enrollment figures alone cannot establish whether a school can meet students' needs.

Appendix A: Disability & Poverty
District leaders have said that, in weighing each scenario, they ask themselves, “Are we asking
our most vulnerable students to carry the load?” We appreciate that question, and we want to
name a hard truth: students with disabilities are already carrying the load of our current system.
This is especially true for disabled students who are also Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual
learners, or experiencing poverty. Ableism does not act alone; it compounds existing inequities.

Under Scenario A, the share of the district’s elementary students with IEPs who attend
high-poverty schools would rise from 33% to 38%. High-poverty schools make up a third of the
remaining elementary schools but would absorb 48% of the students with IEPs added to
receiving schools.




As a result, the proposed scenarios would further concentrate the number of students with IEPs
in high-poverty schools. Today, the number of students with IEPs at the 26 K-5 schools that
would receive students is unrelated to poverty. The largest special education populations are
spread across schools of all income levels. With the proposed changes, the largest populations
shift to high-poverty schools. Those schools already serve higher shares of students with IEPs,
and they are the ones projected to grow the most, including Rosa Parks, Whitman, and Sitton.

Appendix B: Secondary School Options for Disabled Students
Both scenarios propose to eliminate most of PPS’s K-8 middle grades, the middle grades at two
thematic focus options, and MLC’s alternative high school, consolidating students into larger
comprehensive schools. These are the settings that serve the highest shares of students with
disabilities. For many of those students, they are where public education has finally worked. The
proposal treats a smaller, continuous environment as an inefficiency. For some students with
disabilities, it is how they access a free appropriate public education.

We understand that tradeoffs and difficult decisions have to be weighed. But you can’t weigh a
tradeoff without understanding, and acknowledging, what you’re voting to give up. The district’s
case for closing or shrinking these options rests on three claims:

   1.​ They cost more per student than comprehensive middle and high schools.
   2.​ They are subsidized by larger schools.
   3.​ They create unequal access to electives, support services and extracurriculars.

What the district’s analysis leaves out is what these settings provide for students with
disabilities: a calmer sensory environment, continuity with the same caring adults across many
years, and a stable peer group. Research identifies these as protective factors. Students who
feel connected at school are less likely to experience persistent sadness or attempt suicide, and
connectedness declines as schools get larger. Disabled and neurodivergent students face far
higher risks of bullying and suicide. For them, these settings are not a luxury. They are
protection.

The district also suggested that K-8 middle school teachers are overburdened because they
teach multiple subjects. That framing leaves out the other side of the tradeoff: a teacher who
teaches more subjects teaches fewer students. At a comprehensive middle school, a
single-subject teacher may see 150 to 180 students a day, roughly 25 to 30 of them with IEPs. A
K-8 teacher may see 60 to 90, and know them across multiple subjects and often multiple years.

For some students with disabilities, particularly those with sensory needs, anxiety, or trauma
histories, a setting where a few adults know them well is what makes their IEP possible to
implement. It’s how accommodations actually happen, how escalation is caught early, and how
families and teachers stay in regular contact. Some disabled students need these settings to
receive a free appropriate public education.

What PPS’s own data shows

   ●​ Seats lost. Under both scenarios, PPS would lose about 70% of its middle-grade seats
      in K-8 settings, from 1,926 students in 12 schools to 566 in 5. It would also lose nearly
      half of its middle-grade seats in choice and alternative K-8 programs, from 694 to 361.




 SEPTSA | PPS Rightsizing                 October 2026                             Page 23 of 36
  ●​ Who these options serve. The three thematic focus options losing grades or their focus
     program serve the highest shares of students with IEPs among PPS focus options, from
     22% to 31%, compared with a middle school average of 16%. MLC loses its high school,
     Bridger loses its middle grades, and Sunnyside loses both its middle grades and its
     focus program. Options with no proposed changes serve lower shares.




SEPTSA | PPS Rightsizing              October 2026                           Page 24 of 36
   ●​ Attendance gaps. At PPS’s comprehensive middle schools, students with disabilities
      are chronically absent at rates 11 percentage points higher than their schoolmates,
      about twice the gap at K-8 schools (5 points). Because this compares students within the
      same school, it isn’t explained by school poverty. MLC, Sunnyside, and Bridger all have
      gaps below the middle school average.




The cost framing also omits how special education is funded. Oregon’s school funding formula
counts each student on an IEP as more than one student, up to a cap, because educating
students with disabilities is expected to cost more. Schools that serve higher shares of students
with IEPs should be expected to cost more per student. Calling that a “subsidy” treats the cost
of serving disabled students as a burden other students carry. That framing is harmful, and it is
ableist.

What we acknowledge

These settings are not equally accessible, and we don’t want to pretend otherwise:

   ●​ Students with additional support needs. None of the thematic focus options affected
      by this proposal hosts an SES, CB or ISC classroom, so students in focus classroom
      placements can’t attend them.




 SEPTSA | PPS Rightsizing                 October 2026                             Page 25 of 36
   ●​ Harm within these schools. Some disabled students have experienced school trauma
      and exclusionary discipline at these schools, just as disabled students have at every
      PPS school.
   ●​ Students in poverty and underserved racial groups. Focus options serve fewer
      students experiencing poverty and fewer students from historically underserved racial
      groups than the district as a whole.

These inequities are real. Access to thematic focus options has been shaped by district
decisions about where programs are located, how lotteries work, how families learn about them,
whether transportation is provided, and where focus classrooms are placed. Eliminating these
settings does not magically fix that.

Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility Utilization
We often hear that special education is “a service, not a place.” That principle rightly protects
students’ access to general education. But services are delivered somewhere, and for many
students with disabilities, the physical and sensory environment is part of how their disability is
experienced. That is easiest to see for students with mobility or physical disabilities, where ADA
requirements are familiar. It is just as true for disabled neurodivergent learners, who may need a
lower sensory school environment, quiet break space or motor room to regulate, or a contained
outdoor area where they can be safe while accessing nature.

PPS’s own 2021 Long Range Facility Plan recognizes this. It found that at many schools:
  ●​ Special education classrooms are undersized or placed in “leftover” spaces.
  ●​ There is no appropriately equipped accessible restroom or designated safe, quiet space
       for self-regulation.
  ●​ Some multistory buildings lack elevators.
  ●​ Some focus classrooms lack access to water.

It also sets a standard. Every configuration should support at least two classrooms (one with a
sink), a sensory-motor space, a break or wellness space, and an accessible restroom.
Consolidation will concentrate more students with IEPs in fewer buildings. Consider Peninsula,
an Inclusive School Community, which is proposed to close. Today it has 4 Learning Center
rooms, a sensory-motor room, 2 break/wellness spaces, an outdoor sensory garden in a
contained courtyard, and an enclosed outdoor nature area directly off its kindergarten
classrooms. Its students would move to Rosa Parks, where the number of students with IEPs is
projected to roughly triple. A break space that works for 25 students will not work for 50 or more.
These spaces do not appear to be counted in buildings’ functional capacity, so a building can
look like it has room on paper while lacking the spaces students with disabilities need.

Preliminary SEPTSA analysis suggests that receiving schools lack classroom space for
students with disabilities, even at minimum need. We examined 11 elementary schools under
Scenario A: the four Inclusive Community Schools that would absorb another school, and seven
schools that would receive relocated focus classrooms. Seven of the 11 would not have enough
classrooms for their projected 2028-29 students, including all four Inclusive Community Schools.
The estimate counts only minimum needs for illustrative purposes, and does not factor in
access to outdoor spaces:
    ●​ Homerooms at the contract’s maximum class sizes
    ●​ Learning Center rooms after removing estimated speech-only students
    ●​ One sensory-motor room per school
    ●​ Half-size break spaces

Appendix D: Inclusive School Communities
PPS launched Inclusive Community Schools to serve students with disabilities in their
neighborhood schools rather than in separate focus classrooms. The model began at Sitton and
Marysville in 2023-24 and expanded to Astor, César Chávez, Rosa Parks, James John,
Peninsula and Whitman in 2024-25. The district’s stated goal is to continue adding schools until
every PPS student is educated in their neighborhood school or school of choice (Portland Public
Schools, 2026).

The district’s own data shows the model is working to change outcomes for disabled kids. PPS’s
2025-26 progress report on its eight inclusive schools found:
   ●​ Reading: Among autistic students, the share meeting reading expectations rose from
       10.7% in the comparison group to 24.2% in the inclusive model.
   ●​ Attendance: Students in the model had stronger attendance than the comparison group
       on every measure.
   ●​ Assessment participation: Students in the model took district assessments at
       substantially higher rates, which the district calls one of its strongest findings.
   ●​ Climate and belonging: Families at inclusive schools consistently rated school climate
       higher than families at other schools, and students’ sense of belonging matched the
       district average.

The district has also invested heavily in building the model: three rounds of professional learning
for teachers, coaching and site visits from the University of Washington’s Haring Center
monthly principal learning communities, and a new joint team with the teachers’ union.

Both scenarios move in the opposite direction:

   ●​ Three of eight schools close. Marysville, James John and Peninsula would close.
      That’s more than a third of the model’s schools, compared with about one in four
      elementary schools overall. In addition, César Chávez would leave the model.
   ●​ Fewer, larger schools. The remaining neighborhood inclusive schools would go from
      eight to five. Their average enrollment would rise from 307 to 470 students, and their
      average number of students with IEPs from 73 to 123.
