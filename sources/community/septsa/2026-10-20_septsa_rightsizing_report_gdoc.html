PPS Rightsizing: Special Education Impacts and Information Requests

This report was prepared by the PPS Special Education Parent Teacher Student Association (SEPTSA), with input from families, for the PPS Rightsizing Committee, Superintendent, and PPS Board of Education. It is being delivered on Tuesday, October 20, 2026.

Executive Summary

PPS's proposed Rightsizing scenarios raise significant concerns about disproportionate impacts on students with disabilities, particularly those with the highest support needs or who are multiply-marginalized. These concerns extend beyond school closures and focus classroom relocations – they include disabled students receiving services in general education, Learning Centers, Inclusive Schools, and other settings. SEPTSA is requesting the data, analysis, and explanations needed to understand these impacts, assess whether students' needs would be met under either scenario, and evaluate alternatives before decisions are made.

Introduction

Students with disabilities, especially those who are Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual learners, or experiencing poverty, prove their resilience every day. They are more likely to be bullied by peers. They are more likely to be shuffled between placements and buildings, or to be barred from attending school with their siblings and neighbors. And they are more likely to experience exclusionary discipline, from losing recess to being suspended. Still, they (and their families) keep showing up, insisting that their gifts and humanity be seen, and building belonging and community in spite of it all.

This report outlines SEPTSA’s collective concerns and explains why the information PPS has provided is insufficient to determine whether any proposed Rightsizing scenario would preserve equitable access to appropriate educational settings, services, and supports for students with disabilities.

Priority 1: Districtwide Service Inventory and Disability Impact

The district says “services follow students,” so families and the Board need to see the current landscape of special education services and students. The share of students affected by closures doesn’t show whether the plan is equitable for the full range of students with disabilities. That takes a disability-specific analysis of all scenarios, with particular attention to students at the intersections of disability, poverty, race, or multilingual learner status.

⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that the proposal would further concentrate more students with IEPs at higher-poverty receiving schools (see Appendix A: Disability & Poverty).

Students with disabilities are already moved between buildings and placements more than other students, before any rightsizing change. Focus classrooms exist at only 34 of PPS’s 73 elementary, K-8, and middle schools, so most students placed in them attend school outside their neighborhood, apart from siblings and peers. Programs don’t always continue across grade bands at the same site, and students may move between placements. Rightsizing would add new disruptions on top of these existing ones, affecting students in focus classrooms as well as those receiving services through Learning Centers, Inclusive Schools, and general education.

This is especially true for disabled students who are also Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual learners, or experiencing poverty. Ableism does not act alone; it compounds existing inequities. SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that Scenario A would concentrate more students with IEPs at higher-poverty receiving schools, including Rosa Parks, Whitman, and Sitton (see Appendix A: Disability & Poverty). PPS must verify these projections and examine how the proposed changes affect students at the intersection of disability, poverty, race, and multilingual learner status. That analysis needs to look beyond the percent of students changing schools to consider additional transitions, disruptions to existing supports, and access to appropriate educational settings and services under both scenarios.

Deliverable A: Districtwide special education inventory

A school-by-school inventory of special education programs, services, and staffing for 2026-27, along with projected changes under all Rightsizing scenarios for 2027-28 and 2028-29. Include:

  • Program and service inventory: List special education programs and services, including locations, grade bands, classroom configurations, available placements, capacity/ratio guidelines, and allocated staffing.
  • Student counts: Current and projected student counts, with separate totals for IEPs and 504 plans, clear definitions, and consistent denominators. Break out counts by service type: speech-only services, Learning Centers, Inclusive Schools, general education supports, CB/SES/ISC classrooms, regional or itinerant services, and specialized programs such as CRIS for Deaf/Hard of Hearing students.
  • Contact directory: Case manager names and contact information, and the district special education administrator responsible for each school or program.
  • Data transparency: The sources, definitions, and calculations behind the school-specific Rightsizing profiles. Also, how PPS will publicly correct errors or omissions, including missing special education staffing and program information.

Deliverable B: Disability-specific equity and impact analysis

An analysis of how each scenario would affect students with disabilities, with clear data sources, assumptions, and protections for student privacy. Include:

  • Intersectional impact: How disability intersects with poverty, multilingual learner status and race/ethnicity, and any disproportionate impact under each scenario.
  • Distribution impact: How each scenario changes where students with disabilities and special education services are located, geographically and socioeconomically.
  • Disruption impact: School assignments and program relocations, including students assigned differently from their general education peers, such as CRIS students moving from Creston to Glencoe, and changes at schools otherwise considered "unimpacted." Evaluate how proposed moves would affect students' relationships with special education teachers, paraeducators, peers, and other trusted adults, as well as the routines and school environments that help them attend, regulate, and learn. Account for the cumulative impact on students who have already changed schools because of program availability, including the potential loss of supports that reduce their need for additional services.
  • Transportation impact: Distances and ride times, including impacts on students receiving transportation services and those assigned to schools farther from home.
  • Measures of disruption: How PPS evaluates impact beyond the number of students who move, including students assigned farther from home, separated from their school communities, or required to change schools between grade bands.
  • Schools with the greatest change: Schools with the largest projected changes in special education populations and service needs, and the method used to identify them.
  • Alternatives: Options PPS considered or could consider to reduce disproportionate impacts, and why each was adopted or rejected.

  1. Is PPS proposing or considering changes to special education placement criteria, program eligibility, or service delivery models as part of Rightsizing? If so, what changes are being considered, how would they affect students' access to neighborhood schools and specialized programs, and how would individual IEP team placement decisions be protected?

Priority 2: Pathways, Program Continuity, and Transitions

The current proposed scenarios do not attend to the diverse needs of disabled students, and in particular the needs that IEP minutes can’t capture. For some students with disabilities, particularly those with sensory needs, anxiety, or trauma histories, a smaller setting or reduced transitions is what makes their IEP possible to implement.

⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis indicates that the thematic focus options losing grades or their program serve the highest shares of students with IEPs among PPS focus options, from 22% to 31%, compared with a middle school average of 16% (see Appendix B: Secondary School Options for Disabled Students).

Both scenarios propose to eliminate most of PPS’s K-8 middle grades, the middle grades at two thematic focus options, and MLC’s alternative high school, consolidating students into larger comprehensive schools. These are among the settings that serve the highest shares of students with disabilities (see Appendix B: Secondary School Options for Disabled Students). For many of those students, they are where public education has finally worked. The proposal treats a smaller, continuous environment as an inefficiency. For some students with disabilities, it is how they access a free appropriate public education. Disabled and neurodivergent students also face far higher risks of bullying and suicide. For them, these settings are not a luxury. They are protection.

The proposals also leave existing inequities in place. Many students with disabilities can’t attend their neighborhood school. Some focus classrooms don’t offer K-5 continuity, so students change schools between grades 2 and 3. And students in general education, DLI and focus classrooms often follow different school assignments, separating students with disabilities from their school communities and peers.

Deliverable C: Educational pathways and continuity

An evaluation of how both scenarios would affect where students with disabilities attend school, the programs and services available to them, and the number of school transitions they may experience. Address K-8 conversions, the proposed closure of MLC's high school grades, and specialized program relocations. Include:

  • Secondary school options: Map middle and high school pathways for students attending schools outside their neighborhood feeder patterns, including regional programs and the proposed elimination of MLC's high school grades. Identify what alternative educational options would remain.
  • Focus classroom continuity: Identify which focus classrooms offer K-5 continuity and where students must change schools between K-2 and grades 3-5. Evaluate alternatives that reduce unnecessary transitions.
  • Separate pathways: Show how school assignments and transitions differ for students in general education, DLI, and focus classrooms, including situations where students with disabilities are separated from their existing school communities and peers.
  • Service and space comparison: Compare current K-8 and alternative school settings with proposed receiving schools, including staffing, caseloads, service schedules, accessibility, regulation spaces, school size, and instructional and inclusion supports.
  • Outcomes: For K-8, focus and alternative programs compared with comprehensive middle and high schools, report attendance, discipline and academic outcomes for students with IEPs, separately from all students.
  • Transfers: For the thematic focus or alternative programs proposed for closure or grade changes, report how many students with IEPs transferred in from a comprehensive school, and in what grade.
  • Conditions needed to implement students’ IEPs in comprehensive schools: What structures would provide the conditions needed to implement students’ IEPs in comprehensive middle and high schools, such as small learning communities, sensory-supportive spaces and limits on the number of students each teacher serves? Are they planned and funded? If so, how does their cost affect the projected savings from eliminating smaller settings?
  • Alternatives considered: How did PPS evaluate the needs of students with disabilities who benefit from remaining in a K-8 or K-12 setting when developing each scenario? What K-8 options would remain available, and what alternatives did PPS consider to preserve continuity for these students? Before proposing closures, did the district evaluate ways to expand access to smaller settings for students with higher support needs and students experiencing poverty, such as adding focus classrooms, changing lottery and transportation practices, or growing enrollment to lower cost per student? If so, what alternatives were considered, what did the analysis find, and why were they rejected in favor of closure?

Additional Questions

  1. How does PPS currently ensure access to Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs for students with disabilities, including those who require focus classroom placements? What options did PPS consider for providing specialized services within DLI schools when developing each Rightsizing scenario? How would PPS maintain appropriate staffing, language access, and communication with families under each proposal?

Priority 3: Staffing, Caseloads, and Service Capacity

The District presents rightsizing as a path to more stable staffing. Families, staff, and the Board need to know what that means for special education specifically: who will serve students and at what caseloads.

We understand the benefits of a well-resourced school, and we value the proposal's goal: consistent, stable staffing dedicated within each building; balanced class sizes across schools; and access to support staff like counselors and social workers. Consolidating schools could improve access to special education staff and specialists, but PPS needs to show how that would work in practice. Staffing numbers alone don't tell us whether students will receive the services they need. The district must account for vacancies, caseloads, service schedules, general education class sizes, and the support students need throughout the school day.

Deliverable D: Staffing and service capacity

Publish current staffing, vacancies, and caseloads for each school in 2026-27, along with staffing projections under both Rightsizing scenarios for 2027-28 and 2028-29. Identify the additional positions, resources, and funding needed to maintain required services for students already enrolled at receiving schools and those who would be moving into them. Include:

  • Staffing standards: The standards PPS uses to set staffing levels, caseloads and classroom enrollment, distinguishing limits set by contract or law from district allocation formulas. Compare each with projected needs under each scenario.
  • Positions by school, current and forecasted: Budgeted, allocated, and filled full-time equivalent (FTE) positions, vacancies, caseloads, and student-to-staff ratios where applicable. For staff assigned to multiple schools, identify each school served and how their time is allocated. Report this for:
  • Learning Center teachers and paraeducators, including current and projected caseloads and instructional group sizes.
  • CB, SES, and ISC teachers and paraeducators, including classroom counts, enrollment, grade bands, and projected staffing.
  • Qualified mental health professionals (QMHPs), Therapeutic Instructional Coaches, counselors, social workers, psychologists, and other mental health supports.
  • Speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, physical therapists, and other related-service providers, including regional and itinerant staff.
  • Inclusive Schools staffing and support allocations, including projected staffing needs under each Rightsizing scenario, using the PPS Level of Need formulas.
  • General education staffing, current and projected classroom counts and class sizes by school and grade level, including comparisons with PAT Agreement Target and Threshold Sizes.
  • Cost and savings: Projected gross and net savings under each scenario, and how much of the budget shortfall they would address. Confirmation that PPS's Rightsizing staffing presentations and financial projections include special education and related-service personnel, not only general education staffing.

Priority 4: Building Capacity, Space, and Accessibility

The District often says special education is “a service, not a place.” But services are delivered somewhere. Families and staff need assurance that receiving schools will have the classrooms, regulation spaces, and accessible facilities students need, and that services won’t end up in hallways, storage rooms or shared offices.

⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis suggests that many receiving elementary schools may not have enough rooms to meet projected needs (see Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility Utilization).

PPS cannot determine whether a school has room for additional students based on building capacity alone. The district must identify the classrooms, therapy and related-service spaces, accessible restrooms, and sensory regulation areas students need. PPS must also explain how it will preserve these spaces as enrollment increases, rather than converting them into additional classrooms at the expense of students with disabilities.

PPS's Long Range Facility Plan (Portland Public Schools, 2021) identified inadequate special education spaces and established space standards. PPS must explain which standards still apply and how it used them when developing the Rightsizing scenarios. SEPTSA's preliminary analysis suggests that seven of the eleven receiving elementary schools under Scenario A may not have enough rooms to meet projected needs. PPS must verify these findings using current room inventories, existing room uses, projected enrollment, and applicable facility standards (see Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility Utilization).

Deliverable E: Facilities, accessibility, and readiness

A written special education readiness assessment for each receiving school, including current and proposed room-by-room inventories, showing how each space is used now and how PPS proposes to use it under each scenario. PPS must explain how it calculated each school's functional capacity, compare its plans with applicable facility and accessibility standards, and document the alternatives it considered. Include:

  • Functional capacity: How PPS calculates building capacity, including space for Learning Centers, focus classrooms, sensory and regulation supports, related services and other specialized instruction.
  • Space inventory: Dedicated and shared spaces at each school for general education, special education, multilingual learner instruction, interventions, art, music and science, libraries, counseling and related services. Show how consolidation would affect scheduling and existing uses.
  • Regulation spaces: Sensory-motor, calming, quiet, wellness and regulation spaces at each school, including dedicated spaces for SES classrooms and QMHPs. Show how each is used today and whether enough appropriately equipped spaces would remain under each scenario.
  • Accessibility features: Inventory and assess adequacy of accessible restrooms, changing tables, elevators, water access, enclosed outdoor spaces, parking, and safe pickup/drop-off arrangements.
  • Accessibility improvements: Funded improvement plan identifying necessary work, costs, funding sources, and completion timelines.
  • Alignment with 2021 Long Range Facility Plan: Assessment of how facilities compare with the 2021 Long Range Facility Plan standards established for accessible and trauma-informed design.

Additional Questions

  1. How does PPS determine how many quiet and sensory-motor spaces each school needs, and how will it ensure students can access them when needed? Explain how the district accounts for students' sensory and regulation needs beyond the supports explicitly documented in their IEPs.
  2. What alternatives to closure did PPS consider, including installing elevators, improving accessible routes, renovating buildings, or relocating classrooms and services to the first floor? Provide the estimated costs, timelines, and reasons PPS accepted or rejected each option. Explain whether these alternatives could provide students with disabilities access to the full school experience, including classrooms, electives, shared spaces, and school activities.
  3. How did PPS account for completed accessibility improvements and current building conditions when evaluating schools for closure or consolidation? When PPS describes a school as inaccessible or favors a single-story building, what specific accessibility features or barriers support that assessment?

Priority 5: Sensory Impact & Meaningful Inclusion

For many students with disabilities, the sensory environment is part of how their disability is experienced. Larger schools mean more noise, crowding, and transitions. Families and the Board need to know whether and how the district assessed this in selecting closing and receiving schools.

⚠️ SEPTSA's preliminary analysis suggests that proposed enrollment increases could place disproportionate pressure on Inclusive School Communities (see Appendix D: Inclusive School Communities).

Larger schools may offer more resources, but increased noise, crowding, and daily transitions can create barriers for students with disabilities. PPS must evaluate how each scenario would affect students' ability to learn, regulate, build relationships, and participate in school activities. That means examining the staffing, routines, instructional approaches, and inclusion supports that currently work for students, along with what receiving schools could provide. Smaller schools and focus-option programs are not automatically better settings, but PPS must account for what makes them effective for the students they serve.

SEPTSA's preliminary analysis of Scenario A projects that enrollment at four Inclusive Schools receiving communities would average 34% above a 360-student benchmark, compared with 20% above that benchmark at other receiving K-5 schools (see Appendix D: Inclusive School Communities). PPS must verify these calculations and explain how it uses the 360-student benchmark in Rightsizing decisions. The district must also assess how larger enrollments could affect students' sensory environments, access to inclusion supports, and availability of services. Enrollment figures alone cannot establish whether a school can meet students' needs.

Deliverable F: Sensory environments and meaningful inclusion

School-level assessments of sensory impacts, student safety, mental health and well-being, school attendance, and meaningful inclusion under each scenario, including specific mitigation and alternatives considered. Include:

  • How sensory impact was evaluated: How did PPS evaluate sensory impacts when selecting schools for closure or consolidation, projecting enrollment, and considering K-8 conversions? How would increased enrollment affect students with disabilities in PE classes, cafeterias, hallways, and other shared spaces? Publish the evaluation criteria, findings, and how those findings influenced each scenario.
  • Where impacts are greatest: The schools and students where PPS expects the greatest effects on sensory needs, safety and meaningful inclusion under each scenario, and the alternatives and supports it considered to reduce them.

If so, which schools, and how are they affected by the proposed scenarios? Please share the full progress report data by school, and explain how these results were weighed in selecting closing and receiving schools.

What staffing, environmental supports, and other resources would PPS provide to prevent or reduce these impacts under each scenario?

Priority 7: Family & Community Engagement

Families need to know how their feedback is being used. If new or revised scenarios are proposed, they need enough time to understand them and respond.

Parents and caregivers of students with disabilities become case managers, advocates and crisis responders. They navigate meetings, evaluations, insurance systems and often daily school communication to make sure their children’s basic needs are met. This work is time-consuming, emotionally and financially draining, and often invisible, and caregivers of children with disabilities experience disproportionately high rates of depression and anxiety. These families need enough time to understand new information and respond, and they deserve to see that their feedback affects district decisions.

Deliverable H: Review timeline

If PPS introduces an additional scenario or substantially changes an existing proposal, publish the updated disability-specific impact analysis, staffing and capacity projections, and school-specific information, with a mutually agreed upon time for families and the Board to review and respond before decisions are made. Our proposal is as follows:

  • Advance materials: Publish the updated disability-specific impact analysis, staffing and capacity projections, and school-specific information at least 30 days before Board vote.
  • Accessible formats: Publish materials following WCAG digital accessibility standards and in plain language, on the same day information is released to the public.
  • Dedicated session: Hold at least one public session focused on special education impacts during each review period, with an option for remote participation and with a posted recording.

Deliverable I: Documented family engagement

Explain how PPS has evaluated family input, what changes it has made to the proposals in response, and how that feedback will inform final recommendations and decisions. Include:

  • Who was heard: The number of engagement sessions, public comments, and survey responses from families of students with disabilities, showing race, poverty, and language intersectionality whenever possible while maintaining privacy.
  • What families said: The main themes in that input.

Priority 8: Transparency & Accountability

Families and the Board need to understand how the rights, outcomes, and wellbeing of students with disabilities were considered, and what alternatives were weighed to reduce the impact on a community already underserved. Going forward, the district needs a public way to answer these questions and track its commitments.

Our report raises concerns about the impact of rightsizing on students with disabilities, and those impacts would fall hardest on the most vulnerable students in PPS. Our community understands that doing nothing has costs too. But as the scenarios stand, we are alarmed at what they would mean for disabled students’ rights, outcomes, and wellbeing. When students’ needs for staff, space, and a manageable sensory environment go unmet, the result is predictable: dysregulation, school refusal, and exclusionary discipline, all of which mean more missed school, worse academic outcomes, and harm to students’ mental and behavioral health.

Families, staff and communities need to know how the district weighed the needs of students with disabilities, what alternatives it considered, and how it will answer the questions in this report before decisions are made.

Deliverable J: Scenario methodology and alternatives

A public explanation of how each scenario was developed, and how the rights, outcomes, and wellbeing of students with disabilities were considered, including:

  • Selection criteria: The criteria and weighting PPS used to select schools for closure, consolidation or reassignment, and how special education enrollment, student support needs, academic outcomes, family preferences and the success of existing programs were considered.
  • Educational settings: How PPS evaluated different settings for students with disabilities, including smaller K-8 schools, alternative programs, comprehensive middle schools, Inclusive School Communities, blended classrooms and specialized classrooms.
  • Facility assessments: How receiving schools were assessed, including how the varied needs of students with disabilities were considered. Where PPS cites accessibility, building configuration, seismic or other facility limitations as reasons to close a school, the supporting assessments, and whether the same criteria were applied to closing and receiving schools. Make all assessments and criteria available for public review.
  • Alternatives to closure: Options PPS considered for schools with underused space, such as adding preschool programs, relocating district services or sharing buildings with community organizations, with their effects on enrollment, operating costs and the ability to preserve smaller school environments.
  • Net savings: The ongoing costs of closed buildings (maintenance, security, utilities and other obligations); how retaining, selling, leasing or repurposing them affects net savings under each scenario; and how PPS weighed those savings against the educational impact of closing schools that currently meet students’ needs.

  • Advance materials: Published at least 3 days before the presentation, in plain language and following WCAG digital accessibility standards.
  • Time for response: At least 60 minutes for public questions, with remote participation and interpretation, and a recording posted within 3 days.

Appendix A: Disability & Poverty

District leaders have said that, in weighing each scenario, they ask themselves, “Are we asking our most vulnerable students to carry the load?” We appreciate that question, and we want to name a hard truth: students with disabilities are already carrying the load of our current system. This is especially true for disabled students who are also Black, Native, Latino/a/e, multilingual learners, or experiencing poverty. Ableism does not act alone; it compounds existing inequities.

Under Scenario A, the share of the district’s elementary students with IEPs who attend high-poverty schools would rise from 33% to 38%. High-poverty schools make up a third of the remaining elementary schools but would absorb 48% of the students with IEPs added to receiving schools.

As a result, the proposed scenarios would further concentrate the number of students with IEPs in high-poverty schools. Today, the number of students with IEPs at the 26 K-5 schools that would receive students is unrelated to poverty. The largest special education populations are spread across schools of all income levels. With the proposed changes, the largest populations shift to high-poverty schools. Those schools already serve higher shares of students with IEPs, and they are the ones projected to grow the most, including Rosa Parks, Whitman, and Sitton.

This report reflects preliminary analysis by SEPTSA members using publicly available PPS data. It has not been verified by the district. We welcome PPS’s corrections and clarifications, and we ask the district to provide its own intersectional analysis of the impact on students with disabilities.

Appendix B: Secondary School Options for Disabled Students

Both scenarios propose to eliminate most of PPS’s K-8 middle grades, the middle grades at two thematic focus options, and MLC’s alternative high school, consolidating students into larger comprehensive schools. These are the settings that serve the highest shares of students with disabilities. For many of those students, they are where public education has finally worked. The proposal treats a smaller, continuous environment as an inefficiency. For some students with disabilities, it is how they access a free appropriate public education.

We understand that tradeoffs and difficult decisions have to be weighed. But you can’t weigh a tradeoff without understanding, and acknowledging, what you’re voting to give up. The district’s case for closing or shrinking these options rests on three claims:

  1. They cost more per student than comprehensive middle and high schools.
  2. They are subsidized by larger schools.
  3. They create unequal access to electives, support services and extracurriculars.

What the district’s analysis leaves out is what these settings provide for students with disabilities: a calmer sensory environment, continuity with the same caring adults across many years, and a stable peer group. Research identifies these as protective factors. Students who feel connected at school are less likely to experience persistent sadness or attempt suicide, and connectedness declines as schools get larger. Disabled and neurodivergent students face far higher risks of bullying and suicide. For them, these settings are not a luxury. They are protection.

The district also suggested that K-8 middle school teachers are overburdened because they teach multiple subjects. That framing leaves out the other side of the tradeoff: a teacher who teaches more subjects teaches fewer students. At a comprehensive middle school, a single-subject teacher may see 150 to 180 students a day, roughly 25 to 30 of them with IEPs. A K-8 teacher may see 60 to 90, and know them across multiple subjects and often multiple years.

For some students with disabilities, particularly those with sensory needs, anxiety, or trauma histories, a setting where a few adults know them well is what makes their IEP possible to implement. It’s how accommodations actually happen, how escalation is caught early, and how families and teachers stay in regular contact. Some disabled students need these settings to receive a free appropriate public education.

What PPS’s own data shows

  • Seats lost. Under both scenarios, PPS would lose about 70% of its middle-grade seats in K-8 settings, from 1,926 students in 12 schools to 566 in 5. It would also lose nearly half of its middle-grade seats in choice and alternative K-8 programs, from 694 to 361.

  • Who these options serve. The three thematic focus options losing grades or their focus program serve the highest shares of students with IEPs among PPS focus options, from 22% to 31%, compared with a middle school average of 16%. MLC loses its high school, Bridger loses its middle grades, and Sunnyside loses both its middle grades and its focus program. Options with no proposed changes serve lower shares.

  • Attendance gaps. At PPS’s comprehensive middle schools, students with disabilities are chronically absent at rates 11 percentage points higher than their schoolmates, about twice the gap at K-8 schools (5 points). Because this compares students within the same school, it isn’t explained by school poverty. MLC, Sunnyside, and Bridger all have gaps below the middle school average.

The cost framing also omits how special education is funded. Oregon’s school funding formula counts each student on an IEP as more than one student, up to a cap, because educating students with disabilities is expected to cost more. Schools that serve higher shares of students with IEPs should be expected to cost more per student. Calling that a “subsidy” treats the cost of serving disabled students as a burden other students carry. That framing is harmful, and it is ableist.

What we acknowledge

These settings are not equally accessible, and we don’t want to pretend otherwise:

  • Students with additional support needs. None of the thematic focus options affected by this proposal hosts an SES, CB or ISC classroom, so students in focus classroom placements can’t attend them.
  • Harm within these schools. Some disabled students have experienced school trauma and exclusionary discipline at these schools, just as disabled students have at every PPS school.
  • Students in poverty and underserved racial groups. Focus options serve fewer students experiencing poverty and fewer students from historically underserved racial groups than the district as a whole.

These inequities are real. Access to thematic focus options has been shaped by district decisions about where programs are located, how lotteries work, how families learn about them, whether transportation is provided, and where focus classrooms are placed. Eliminating these settings does not magically fix that.

This report reflects preliminary analysis by SEPTSA members using publicly available PPS data. It has not been verified by the district. We welcome PPS’s corrections and clarifications, and we ask the district to provide its own analysis of the outcomes of students with disabilities in K-8, focus, and alternative programs.

Appendix C: Disability Visibility in Facility Utilization

We often hear that special education is “a service, not a place.” That principle rightly protects students’ access to general education. But services are delivered somewhere, and for many students with disabilities, the physical and sensory environment is part of how their disability is experienced. That is easiest to see for students with mobility or physical disabilities, where ADA requirements are familiar. It is just as true for disabled neurodivergent learners, who may need a lower sensory school environment, quiet break space or motor room to regulate, or a contained outdoor area where they can be safe while accessing nature.

PPS’s own 2021 Long Range Facility Plan recognizes this. It found that at many schools:

  • Special education classrooms are undersized or placed in “leftover” spaces.
  • There is no appropriately equipped accessible restroom or designated safe, quiet space for self-regulation.
  • Some multistory buildings lack elevators.
  • Some focus classrooms lack access to water.

It also sets a standard. Every configuration should support at least two classrooms (one with a sink), a sensory-motor space, a break or wellness space, and an accessible restroom.

Consolidation will concentrate more students with IEPs in fewer buildings. Consider Peninsula, an Inclusive School Community, which is proposed to close. Today it has 4 Learning Center rooms, a sensory-motor room, 2 break/wellness spaces, an outdoor sensory garden in a contained courtyard, and an enclosed outdoor nature area directly off its kindergarten classrooms. Its students would move to Rosa Parks, where the number of students with IEPs is projected to roughly triple. A break space that works for 25 students will not work for 50 or more. These spaces do not appear to be counted in buildings’ functional capacity, so a building can look like it has room on paper while lacking the spaces students with disabilities need.

Preliminary SEPTSA analysis suggests that receiving schools lack classroom space for students with disabilities, even at minimum need. We examined 11 elementary schools under Scenario A: the four Inclusive Community Schools that would absorb another school, and seven schools that would receive relocated focus classrooms. Seven of the 11 would not have enough classrooms for their projected 2028-29 students, including all four Inclusive Community Schools. The estimate counts only minimum needs for illustrative purposes, and does not factor in access to outdoor spaces:

  • Homerooms at the contract’s maximum class sizes
  • Learning Center rooms after removing estimated speech-only students
  • One sensory-motor room per school
  • Half-size break spaces

This report reflects preliminary analysis by SEPTSA members using publicly available PPS data. It has not been verified by the district. We welcome PPS’s corrections and clarifications, and we ask the district to provide its own analysis of the building-level inventory of spaces that serve disabled students.

Appendix D: Inclusive School Communities

PPS launched Inclusive Community Schools to serve students with disabilities in their neighborhood schools rather than in separate focus classrooms. The model began at Sitton and Marysville in 2023-24 and expanded to Astor, César Chávez, Rosa Parks, James John, Peninsula and Whitman in 2024-25. The district’s stated goal is to continue adding schools until every PPS student is educated in their neighborhood school or school of choice (Portland Public Schools, 2026).

The district’s own data shows the model is working to change outcomes for disabled kids. PPS’s 2025-26 progress report on its eight inclusive schools found:

  • Reading: Among autistic students, the share meeting reading expectations rose from 10.7% in the comparison group to 24.2% in the inclusive model.
  • Attendance: Students in the model had stronger attendance than the comparison group on every measure.
  • Assessment participation: Students in the model took district assessments at substantially higher rates, which the district calls one of its strongest findings.
  • Climate and belonging: Families at inclusive schools consistently rated school climate higher than families at other schools, and students’ sense of belonging matched the district average.

The district has also invested heavily in building the model: three rounds of professional learning for teachers, coaching and site visits from the University of Washington’s Haring Center

monthly principal learning communities, and a new joint team with the teachers’ union.

Both scenarios move in the opposite direction:

  • Three of eight schools close. Marysville, James John and Peninsula would close. That’s more than a third of the model’s schools, compared with about one in four elementary schools overall. In addition, César Chávez would leave the model.
  • Fewer, larger schools. The remaining neighborhood inclusive schools would go from eight to five. Their average enrollment would rise from 307 to 470 students, and their average number of students with IEPs from 73 to 123.

The Inclusive School Communities would grow furthest beyond PPS’s size goal. Under Scenario A, the four that would absorb another school would average 34% above the 360-student goal, compared with 20% for the other 22 receiving K-5 schools. All four exceed that average, led by Arleta at 49%. These schools serve the highest shares of students with IEPs among receiving schools. The students most affected by crowding, noise, and transitions would be concentrated in the schools growing the most.

The district’s last consolidation shows the risk. PPS’s own equity audit of its 2023 Southeast enrollment changes found schools serving more students in special education than projected, and staff “trying to implement an inclusive neighborhood model without enough paraeducator and special education support” (Green & Hanson, 2026, p. 15). Families described promised supports that never fully materialized. The current proposals ask a fragile new model to absorb more students, more need, and more change at once. It also risks blaming the model, and the students it serves, for the effects of under-resourcing and consolidation, as we saw happen at Peninsula last year.

This report reflects preliminary analysis by SEPTSA members using publicly available PPS data. It has not been verified by the district. We welcome PPS’s corrections and clarifications, and we ask the district to provide its own analysis of the impact on Inclusive School Communities.
