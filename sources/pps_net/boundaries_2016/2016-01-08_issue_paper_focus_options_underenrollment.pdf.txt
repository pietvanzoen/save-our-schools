              SYSTEM PLANNING AND PERFORMANCE - PORTLAND PUBLIC SCHOOLS

                              FOCUS OPTION REVIEW PART I: IMPACT OF FOCUS OPTIONS ON
                              UNDER-ENROLLMENT AT NEIGHBORHOOD SCHOOLS




In 2013, the Superintendent’s Advisory Committee for Enrollment & Transfer (SACET) was tasked with
developing recommendations to better align enrollment and transfer policies with the Portland Public
Schools’ (PPS) strategic framework, and Racial Educational Equity Policy. In keeping with the
Superintendent’s charge, SACET concentrated on focus option schools as described in the Education
Options policy (6.10.022-P). Among the recommendations made by the committee in 2014 was to
implement a quality review of District focus option schools, including dual language immersion
programs, with the following objectives: 1) an explanation of the purpose of each focus option and the
development of measures of success and an evaluation process (Recommendation 3.1 and 3.2) and 2) an
examination of focus option locations as part of the district-wide boundary review, now called
enrollment balancing (Recommendation 3.3).

This issue paper contains preliminary analysis conducted by the System Planning and Performance
department (SPP) to support the Education Options Review committee, the group charged with
conducting the focus option review. This analysis will also be provided to the District-wide Boundary
Review Advisory Committee (DBRAC) to assist with enrollment balancing and specifically with the goal of
sizing neighborhood schools in order to provide equitable core programming to all students.

This analysis addresses one theme: would changing the mix of focus options or how students access
them alleviate under-enrollment or over-crowding at neighborhood schools? The role charter schools
and community-based alternatives plays will also be reviewed. Specific questions to be addressed
include:

   1. Are there situations where focus option schools are contributing to under-enrollment at
      neighborhood schools?
   2. Are there situations where re-thinking the locations of focus option schools could help to
      alleviate under-enrollment or over-crowding at neighborhood schools?
   3. If fewer students attended focus options in the Southeast, where we see a disproportionate
      number of focus option schools, would neighborhood schools in the Southeast become over-
      crowded?
   4. If there are focus option or alternative schools that should change locations to alleviate under-
      enrollment or over-crowding issues, what buildings might be able to support these schools?


                               EXECUTIVE SUMMARY – KEY FINDINGS


   •    Relocating focus options is not a primary driver in alleviating under-enrollment: There are a
       number of schools where the building cannot accommodate sufficient enrollment for its current
       configuration. Even if fewer students were enrolled in focus option schools, it would not address
       under-enrollment issues. This is true for Creston, Skyline, Marysville, Lee and potentially Vestal.

   •   However, there is a risk to compounding current under-enrollment problems by re-locating
       focus options to the North or Northeast due to the preponderance of Charter schools in these
       areas: While the North and Northeast has few focus options, it has many charter schools.
       Charter schools are an important factor in potential under-enrollment at neighborhood schools,
       especially in those neighborhoods. Locating focus options in the North or Northeast could
       potentially drain further enrollment from neighborhood schools, especially given the
       preponderance of charter schools in the area.
   •   Dual language immersion programs draw large numbers of students away from higher poverty
       under-enrolled schools; however, DLI aims to serve underserved populations. Dual language
       programs benefit underserved students, especially English Language Learners, and should be
       located in areas where native speakers of the partner language reside.
   •   For schools in the Southeast, the disproportionate number of educational options may be
       alleviating overcrowding. Moving focus options out of the Southeast may increase overcrowding
       at some neighborhood schools.
   •   Relocating thematic focus option schools and other educational options has potential in certain
       cases to simultaneously alleviate over-crowding and under-enrollment
           • ACCESS: Relocating this alternative program could alleviate overcrowding on the split
               campus Beverly Cleary school.
           • Alliance: There may be potential for Alliance HS to become a single rather than split
               campus school, which could free the Meek site for a different educational option.
           • Creative Science: Relocating Creative Science could alleviate over-crowding and under-
               enrollment in the Southeast if the current site converts to a neighborhood elementary
               school feeding to a reconfigured middle school (such as Harrison Park).
           • Metropolitan Learning Center (MLC): The MLC site could hold a 2-section K5 and could
               alleviate overcrowding at Chapman, but could result in the creation of two smaller K5s
               that would be at higher risk of not being able to sustainably offer the core program due to
               their small size.
           • Odyssey: Odyssey no longer fits into Hayhurst and needs a new location. Removing
               Odyssey program from the Hayhurst facility, however, will result in under-enrollment at
               Hayhurst. There may be opportunity on the west side to increase the size of Hayhurst’s
               boundary, while also reducing over-crowding at Chapman.
           • Richmond: It is not necessary to relocate Richmond in order to right-size neighborhood
               schools. PPS could relocate the program and still find a solution resulting in right-sized
               neighborhood schools within the region if there were other important reasons to do so.
           • Sunnyside: It is not necessary to relocate the Sunnyside Environmental focus option to
               right-size neighborhood schools. It is possible to develop a solution where Sunnyside
               becomes a neighborhood school or focus option-only school if there are other critical
               reasons for this change. However, there is not the opportunity for Sunnyside
               Environmental to expand its district wide enrollment due to the neighborhood
               enrollment.
           • Winterhaven: Winterhaven’s building would not support a neighborhood 2 section K-8
               and therefore, does not assist in alleviating over-crowding or under-enrollment at
               neighborhood schools.

                                               METHOD

As part of enrollment balancing, we wanted to better understand the impact of focus options on the
enrollment of neighborhood schools. We believe this analysis is especially timely given the primary goal
of Enrollment Balancing: to right-size neighborhood schools in order to provide equitable core
programming to students. This analysis focuses only on neighborhood schools that are under-enrolled
or nearly under-enrolled—because it is these schools that have the most difficulty sustainably offering
the core program—and on overcrowded neighborhood schools, as these schools face the most difficulty
in having the physical space necessary to offer the core program.

October 1, 2015 data was reviewed to determine if and how much focus option schools are affecting
enrollment at the most under-enrolled schools. Other factors that might be contributing to a school’s
low enrollment were reviewed. With the exception of Alliance and MLC, this analysis did not focus on
high school focus option schools or programs.

The focus of this analysis was overcrowding and under-enrollment at neighborhood schools. K-5 and K-
8 Schools are considered under-enrolled if enrollment per grade is below 50 students per grade. Schools
are considered overcrowded if their facility utilization (the number of classrooms divided by the number
of teachers) is greater than 105%.

Schools may be both overcrowded and under-enrolled if they have both fewer than 50 students were
grade and have too few classrooms to accommodate these students.

                         OVERVIEW OF CURRENT EDUCATIONAL OPTIONS

Types of Educational Options

Several types of programs and schools might be considered in-district “educational options”. For the
purposes of this review, the following types of schools are considered focus options:

Dual Language Immersion (DLI)

These programs offer instruction in both English and a partner language. The priority of DLI programs
are to close opportunity gaps for historically underserved students. PPS offers programs in Spanish,
Japanese, Mandarin Chinese, Russian, and Vietnamese. Currently, all DLI programs are co-located with a
neighborhood school except for Richmond, which is a standalone immersion school. Families apply for
their students to attend DLI programs. Richmond, Ainsworth, Woodstock (in the process of converting
to a two-way program) and West Sylvan are one-way dual language immersion programs that serve
primarily English native speakers. All other DLI schools are two-way immersion programs that aim to


SPP –Focus Option Analysis                                                                        Page 3
serve both minority language and majority language students with each group modeling their native
language for the other. James John, Rigler, Scott, and Sitton DLI programs serve only neighborhood
families; no transfer slots are open to students from other neighborhoods.

Thematic Focus Options

These programs offer a unique curriculum around a theme. PPS currently offers programs that are both
standalone and co-located with neighborhood schools in themes such as art, science, history, career and
technical education. Two focus options, Sunnyside and Buckman, draw enrollment primarily from their
neighborhoods with some spots made available for out-of-neighborhood applicants.

Alternative Options

For the purposes of this report and the educational option review, we are also including in-district
alternatives including ACCESS, Alliance High School, and MLC. These options are not part of the PPS
lottery; instead they have separate requirements (such as academic achievement and demonstrated
need for alternative learning environment) and admission procedures.

Focus Option Siting: A Closer Look at the Southeast

In this section, we address the question: If fewer students attended focus options in the Southeast (e.g.,
if focus options were re-sited elsewhere), where we see a disproportionate number of focus option
schools, would Southeast neighborhood schools become overcrowded?

If all 146 students attending focus options and alternatives were to instead enroll at Bridger, their
average enrollment per grade would jump from about 2 sections (average of 52.9 students per grade) to
close to 3 sections per grade (average of 69.1 students per grade). These additional students need

cause Bridger to become overcrowded. Nearby focus options and alternatives play a role in alleviating
Bridger’s overcrowding.

                 ARE THERE EDUCATIONAL OPTIONS THAT COULD BE CHANGED
                             TO ALLEVIATE OVERCROWDING?

In some situations, moving educational options may alleviate overcrowding in co-located or nearby
neighborhood schools. The next section summarizes opportunities and challenges at focus option and
alternative sites.

ACCESS

ACCESS is a district-wide alternative program serving students with specific social and academic needs; it
occupies 16 classrooms in the Rose City Park site, which is shared with Beverly Cleary, an overcrowded

SPP –Focus Option Analysis                                                                        Page 11
school split across Hollyrood, Fernwood, and Rose City Park campuses. Relocating ACCESS could alleviate
overcrowding at Beverly Cleary through grade reconfiguration.

Alliance

Alliance High School is split across Benson and Meek campuses. It serves a high percentage of
underserved students and may need to be relocated if they are not provided space as part of the Benson
High School bond. To better serve the populations in these programs, a centrally located separate
building or wing would be preferable.

Creative Science School

Relocating this focus option could open the Clark building to become an elementary school that could
feed to a reconfigured middle school in the SE. These changes could alleviate overcrowding at Harrison
Park and the nearly-overcrowded Bridger.

Metropolitan Learning Center (MLC)

This district-wide alternative serves students with specific educational needs. The MLC site is large
enough to hold a 2-section K5 and is located near overcrowded Chapman ES. However, boundary
changes alone would be enough to alleviate overcrowding in this region. Adding a new elementary
school to this region would create two smaller, 2-section K5s that would be less able to absorb
fluctuations in enrollment. Additionally, a similar sized site (approximately 21 rooms) would be needed
to house MLC.

Odyssey

The K8 Odyssey program no longer fits at Hayhurst and needs a new location. Relocating Odyssey would
result in under-enrollment at Hayhurst. There may be opportunities to increase the size of Hayhurst’s
boundary while reducing overcrowding at Chapman.

Richmond

It is not necessary to relocate Richmond solely to right size nearby neighborhood schools. However, PPS
could relocate the program and find a solution that would result in right-sized neighborhood schools if
there were other important reasons to do so.

Sunnyside Environmental

It is not necessary to relocate Sunnyside solely to balance enrollment at nearby neighborhood schools.
Like Richmond, PPS could relocate the focus option and find a solution that would result in right-sized
neighborhood schools if there were other important reasons to do so.
