                         Board Engagement Summary at Kellogg Middle School
                                           April 11, 2026

Between 84-164 respondents answered each Slido question during the engagement session held at
Kellogg Middle School on April 11. While over 40 school communities were represented, Creston
Elementary School, Lee Elementary School, and Chief Joseph Elementary were the most widely
represented schools at this engagement session, and approximately 85% of participants were part of a
K-5 school community., 4% identified as African American/Black, 3% identified as Asian, 3% identified as
Middle Eastern/North African, 2% identified as American Indian/Alaska Native, 78% of participants
identified as white, 8% as Hispanic/Latinx, 9% identified as multiracial. Nearly a third of participants had
students in focus options. A quarter of families experienced a boundary change in the last five years.

Of the six factors in the PPS student assignment policy, respondents prioritized compact boundaries,
right pathways, and program balance more frequently than they prioritized impact, school
demographics, and optimal use of facilities. (See Chart 1 for a breakdown of responses to this question).

When asked to rank four factors that make a positive school environment, respondents identified a wide
variety of learning opportunities, a warm and welcoming environment, access to services that meet all
students' learning needs, and school diversity over ability of children who live close together to attend
the same schools and ability to walk/bike to school. (See Chart 2 for a breakdown of responses to this
question).

When asked what helped students feel connected as they move from one school to the next, 84
participants said they feel most connected during school transitions when they can stay with
neighborhood friends and established peer cohorts. Additionally, frequent turnover in school leadership
can cause significant stress. Ultimately, a successful move is supported by geographic proximity and
intentional transition efforts, such as early school visits, neighborhood-based activities, and strong
parent-teacher involvement that fosters a sense of belonging before the first day of school.

If families reported choosing to change schools in the last five years (88), they did so primarily for
specific academic programs like arts-integrated programs, STEM tracks, or language immersion. Many
also moved out of necessity, seeking increased support for special needs or more manageable classroom
environments when they felt their previous school lacked the resources to ensure physical and
emotional safety. Beyond academics, transitions were driven by practical life changes, including
neighborhood moves for housing affordability, a desire for shorter commutes, or a shift from private to
public education for financial reasons and stronger local community ties.

Finally, respondents were asked what else leaders should know about this process. There is a strong
demand for an equity-centered approach that prioritizes Title 1 schools, which provide essential social
services, food pantries, and Migra Watch programs for migrant families.

Community members also expressed concern that consolidation will destroy the social and academic
safety nets provided by neighborhood schools. Stakeholders emphasize that schools like Creston, Chief
Joseph, Lent, and Buckman serve as vital community hubs, and that closing them would damage social
cohesion. Families also warned that constant threat of school change is upending and mentally taxing,
which drives families toward private or charter schools.

Finally, a major point of frustration is the lack of diverse representation in the decision-making process.
Many noted that district meetings are often dominated by loudest voices (typically white, affluent
parents), while working-class, non-English speaking, and marginalized families are unable to attend.
Participants offered the recommendation that district leaders go directly to the school buildings to hear
from those most impacted.
