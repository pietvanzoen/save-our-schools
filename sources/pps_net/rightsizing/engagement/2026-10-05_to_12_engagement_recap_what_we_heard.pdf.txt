                           Rightsizing Engagement Recap: What We Heard
                                         October 5 - 12, 2026

Executive Summary
This memo is a weekly summary created by the Data & Accountability team to share public feedback on
the proposed Rightsizing Scenarios. It covers community input gathered between October 5 and
October 12, 2026. These weekly reports exist to make the decision-making process open and
transparent.

During the week of October 5–12, Portland Public Schools hosted two public engagement sessions:
    ●​ Board of Education Meeting | October 6th.
    ●​ Board of Education Community Connection | October 9th

Scenarios were released on October 5th, and presented to the public at the Board meeting on October
6th. A Rightsizing Community Survey was also released on October 5th. As of October 12th, 1,132 people
have completed the Rightsizing Scenario Feedback Survey, representing every school community in the
district. About 94% of respondents are connected to a school directly impacted by proposed closures,
boundary changes, or program moves. Parents and guardians made up 73.8% of respondents.

Feedback from open-ended survey responses and community meetings focused on two primary areas:

   1.​ Support and Resources: Families emphasize that safe transportation (district buses and crossing
       assistance on busy streets) is essential. Parents want students to stay with current classmates
       and teachers, and working families need guaranteed, affordable aftercare. Respondents also
       want equal facilities (air conditioning, modern technology, safe playgrounds), moving funds, and
       full-time counselors.
   2.​ Educational Equity: Many respondents feel the plans place an unfair burden of closures and
       boundary changes on low-income and historically marginalized communities in East and
       Northeast Portland while protecting higher-income Westside schools. Some families express
       concern that moving Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs causes language and racial
       segregation.

We also present common question themes emerging from community responses, including about school
selection criteria, geographic equity, how students will get to school, information about financial
transparency, staffing and specialized programs, and a desire for more information bout the enrollment
forecasting process.

Introduction
Every Monday, from when the rightsizing plans were released on October 5th until Superintendent
Armstrong gives her recommendation to the board, the Data and Accountability team will gather and
summarize public feedback. The purpose of these reports is to be open and clear. We want to share
what community members have told us and explain how we will use that information. This memo shares
what we have heard from our community between October 5 - 12, 2026.

Scenario Feedback Survey Findings
As of October 12th, we have received 1,132 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities! About 94% of respondents
represented school communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including
school closure, school consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes. See Tables 1-3 for a

Open-Ended Response Analysis:
The survey asked the following open-ended questions:
    ●​ What specific support (e.g., transportation to school, students staying with their classmates)
        would you need if school changes occur all at once instead of over several years?
    ●​ What specific resources or help should the district provide to support schools affected by
        rightsizing?
    ●​ Our goal is to create a model that is fair and equitable to all communities. Did we miss any
        important equity issues, community history, or student needs in creating these models? If yes,
        please tell us what we missed and how we can address it.
    ●​ What is one thing you would like us to know about your school community?

Below, we present emerging findings from all open-ended questions across four themes: supports and
resources, equity analysis, emerging themes, and emerging questions.

Supports and Resources
Community members say safe transportation is a must if school boundaries change or closures happen.
Families need district busing, safe walking and biking routes, and crossing support at busy streets.
Parents also want students to stay with their current classmates, friends, and trusted teachers to protect
their emotional well-being. Additionally, working parents need expanded and affordable before- and
after-school childcare, like SUN programs and aftercare, to manage longer daily commutes.

To support schools receiving new students, respondents want equal facility standards across all
buildings. Receiving schools should all have working air conditioning, modern playgrounds, up-to-date
classroom technology, and dedicated moving funds so schools are not overwhelmed. Families also want
full-time counselors, mental health services, and transition specialists to help students handle emotional
stress and adjust to a new school environment.

Some community members say that no district resources can justify closing a neighborhood school.
These families see district offers as temporary band-aids that fail to fix the damage caused by closures.
Others worry that the district cannot maintain these supports over time, pointing to budget cuts. Finally,
some respondents argue against spending money on widespread busing or transition support, insisting
that all funds should go directly toward keeping teachers in classrooms.

Equity Analysis
Community members worry that the proposed changes place an unfair burden on East and Northeast
Portland. They worry that historically marginalized and lower-income neighborhood schools face the
majority of closures, boundary shifts, and program moves. Families point out a geographic double
standard, asking why some Westside and higher-income schools remain protected while Eastside Title I
and racially diverse communities bear the brunt of these disruptions.

Another major concern is the relocation and grouping of Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs. Some
families argue that moving these programs isolates students rather than integrating them into local
communities. Some parents worry this move creates racial and language segregation by pulling DLI
students away from their local neighborhood peers.

Respondents worry that geographic and commute challenges will create safety risks, particularly for
families living east of 82nd Avenue. Students may have longer travel distances and lose access to
walkable neighborhood schools. Parents are concerned about children having to cross dangerous major

streets and high-traffic corridors (e.g., 82nd Avenue, Powell Boulevard, MLK Jr. Boulevard, Fremont
Street, and Broadway) without guaranteed busing or crossing guards.

For neurodivergent students and those with Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) or 504 plans,
closing smaller schools creates serious disruption. Smaller environments, like K–8 schools, offer
predictable daily routines and crucial sensory safety nets that help these students succeed. Removing
these supportive settings and transferring students to larger, unfamiliar schools may disrupt established
support structures and cause stress.

Some respondents reject the district’s equity framing entirely, arguing that prioritizing demographic
formulas over academic quality, safety, and school stability hurts strong programs and drives families out
of the district. Others contend that true equity requires equal facility investments and high academic
standards across all existing schools rather than redrawing boundaries or closing campuses.

Other Emerging Themes
A major theme across community responses is widespread distrust in district leadership. Many parents
are concerned that the feedback process is just for show and that final decisions were made before
public input began. To address these concerns, community members demand transparent financial data
showing real cost savings compared to transition expenses, clear criteria for why specific schools were
picked for closure, and updated enrollment forecasts that include new housing growth.

Working parents also express deep alarm over losing critical non-scenario programs, particularly
subsidized before- and after-school childcare like SUN programs. Because school closures and boundary
changes may create longer commutes, families anticipate relying on these services to keep working.

Many respondents also acknowledged that enrollment drops from lower birth rates are real and that
running half-empty buildings is financially unsustainable. These respondents agree that consolidation is
necessary to protect overall district funding. Additionally, some community members express frustration
with pushback against every proposal, urging district leaders to act decisively and finalize rightsizing
plans quickly instead of dragging out uncertainty over several years.

Emerging Questions
Community members brought forth many questions about the proposed scenarios. Common questions
themes (with examples) include:
   ●​ Selection Criteria & Geographic Equity: e.g., "What specific, objective criteria were used to
       designate schools like Sellwood Middle School, Sabin, Maplewood, or James John for closure,
       and why do lower-enrollment Westside campuses appear protected from similar restructuring?"
   ●​ Getting to School: e.g., "How will students safely commute across high-speed traffic
       barriers—such as 82nd Avenue, SE Powell Boulevard, NE Fremont Street, or the St. Johns
       Bridge—without district-provided yellow bus transportation or crossing support?"
   ●​ Financial Transparency: e.g., "Where is the transparent financial breakdown showing net
       operational savings from school closures, and why hasn't central office administrative overhead
       been reduced prior to closing neighborhood schools?"
   ●​ Specialized Program Relocation: e.g., "How will relocated Dual Language Immersion (DLI)
       programs, K–8 configurations, and Special Education (SpEd/504) services maintain their staffing
       ratios and program quality when merged into larger, receiving facilities?"

    ●​ Housing Growth vs. Enrollment Forecasting: e.g., "How do district demographic models
       reconcile declining enrollment projections with active housing construction, transit-oriented
       apartment developments, and affordable housing infill across East and NE Portland?"
    ●​ Transition Timelines & Grandfathering Rules: e.g., "Will current 5th and 8th-grade students be
       grandfathered into their existing middle or high school feeder paths, and will sibling attendance
       policies be guaranteed at receiving schools?"
