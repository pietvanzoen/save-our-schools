                                     Board Engagement Summary:
                                       Virtual Slido Opportunity
                                            May 7-15, 2026


The Office of School Planning and Modernization shared a digital Slido poll to provide an opportunity for
community members who were unable to attend an in-person or virtual engagement session to share
feedback on PPS Rightsizing efforts. This poll was available in English, Spanish, Vietnamese, Chinese,
Russian, and Somali. It was emailed to all members of the PPS community using ParentSquare, and was
open from May 7 to May 15, 2026. This poll was open to anyone, regardless of whether they had
attended an engagement session.

We estimate that about 1620 community members responded to this survey. Between 920 and 1620
people answered each question. About 70% of the people that responded were connected to grades K-5.
Most of the respondents (75%) said their student(s) identify as white. Additionally, 13% of families
identified as Asian, 13% identified as multiracial, 7% identified as Hispanic/Latinx, and 4% identified as
Black/African American. Less than 2% each of respondents identified as American Indian/Alaska Native,
Middle Eastern/North African, and/or Pacific Islander. About 10% of respondents declined to share the
race/ethnicity of their student. About 40% of these families have students inTalented and Gifted
programs. An additional 40% have students in Dual Language Immersion programs. In total, nearly a
quarter (22%) of respondents experienced a school boundary change, a program relocation, or a grade
level change within their school.

The PPS student assignment policy considers six main factors. People picked the three they thought were
most important. The top priorities were feeder patterns that allow as many students as possible to
continue together from one school level to the next, boundaries that promote safer routes to schools, and
program and enrollment stability. (See Chart 1 for the full results).

A total of 920 people answered a question about what helps students feel connected when they move
schools. Families said that staying with the same group of neighborhood friends and classmates as they
move to middle and high school is the most important factor for a child's mental health and safety.
Additionally, the transition between schools is made smoother when principals, teachers, and counselors
reach out early through summer camps, playground visits, and open houses before the school year even
begins. Finally, a school feels like a supportive family when parents can easily volunteer, join PTA events,
use the bike bus, and gather for neighborhood activities that build local trust.

Overall, the feedback showed that families who responded to this Slido want to focus on strong
academics and student safety through this transition. Echoing common themes heard during budget
engagement sessions, some families shared that they think the district has too many top-level managers,
and they want leadership to cut central office salaries before laying off teachers or closing buildings.
Commenters are worried about large class sizes, understaffed recesses, and a lack of support for
students with special needs. While some agree that the district must make hard decisions to rightsize,
others worry that closing neighborhood hubs or specialized language programs will destroy community
trust and cause even more families to leave the district.
