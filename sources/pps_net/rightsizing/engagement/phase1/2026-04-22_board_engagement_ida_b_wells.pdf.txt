                              Board Engagement Summary at Ida B. Wells
                                           April 22, 2026

Between 106-205 respondents answered each Slido question during the engagement session held at Ida
B. Wells on April 22. While over 40 school communities were represented, Rieke Elementary, Bridlemile
Elementary, and Chief Joseph Elementary were the most widely represented schools at this engagement
session, and approximately 90% of participants were part of a K-5 school community. 3% of participants
identified as African American/Black, 1% as American Indian/Alaska Native, 5% identified as Asian, 3% as
Hispanic/Latino/Latinx, 1% as Middle Eastern/North African, 80% identified as white, 11% identified as
multiracial, and 8% declined to answer this question.Nearly half of families in attendance have students
who are identified as talented and gifted. Only 6% of families responding had experienced a boundary
change in the last five years.

Of the six factors in the PPS student assignment policy, respondents prioritized feeder pattern that allows
as many students as possible to continue together from one school level to the next, compact
boundaries that promote safer routes to schools and a sense of community as well as recognize and
address natural and human-made barriers, and program and enrollment stability in the surrounding
schools over other factors.See Chart 1 for a breakdown of responses to this question.

When asked to rank four factors that make a positive school environment, respondents identified a
warm and welcoming school environment, a wide variety of learning opportunities, access to services
that meet every student’s learning need, and ability of children who live close together to attend the
same school as the highest priorities. See Chart 2 for a breakdown of responses to this question.

111 respondents answered the question, “What has helped you/your student feel connected as they
move from one school to the next?” Families shared that support systems in schools are built from a
deep sense of community and continuity, where students thrive through long-term relationships with
neighbors, peers, and school staff. Walkability to school, playground visits, and guided tours help reduce
transition anxiety and create a feeling of physical safety. Additionally, these families value proactive
transparency and engagement, specifically through dedicated staff like climate specialists, high parent
involvement in PTA-led events, and clear communication from school leadership.

Additionally, 106 families shared responses to the question, In the past five years, has your family
experienced a school transition that you initiated? Families that chose to transition school environments
sought out specialized environments that offer academic rigor for gifted students or specific support for
neurodivergent learners that neighborhood schools lacked. Other transitions were driven by
                                                                                                           3

environmental concerns, including high class sizes, unsafe school climates, and funding disparities that
lead parents to consider private alternatives.

Overall, feedback from this listening session emphasized that neighborhood schools provide essential
social threads that connect families and foster student safety. Many parents argue that closing
high-achieving schools like Rieke or unique locations like Skyline would ignore the non-monetary value of
community stability and localized support systems. There is significant concern regarding the potential of
split-feeder patterns, where students are separated from their peer groups during critical developmental
transitions into middle school. Furthermore, families of children in specialized focus programs or K-8
models emphasize that these environments are often the only reason they remain in the public school
system rather than opting for private education.
